# Rarity forecasting is feasible: start with Nutcrackers

A rare-bird "forecast" tab for the Netherlands is feasible as a zero-marginal-cost test site, but not as a copy of the crane radar. The crane radar is a nowcast: it takes "flying over" crane reports that carry a direction, pushes each flock forward for up to four hours with a flight model trained on GPS tracks, and corrects it with KNMI wind forecasts. Rarities are stationary singletons with unpredictable arrival, so that propagation step has nothing to propagate. What the evidence supports is a layered product: a smoothed "activity now" map built from Vogelradar's own reports (persistence with a travel radius and habitat weighting), a climatology prior per species or guild from one to two years of GBIF history, and, only after a backtest proves it, a weather-conditioned arrival layer per guild in the style of the Japanese Ross's Gull forecast. Every input exists free of charge for non-commercial use (Open-Meteo and KNMI wind, CORINE and WorldCover land cover, GBIF history, eBird's notable-observations API for the neighbouring countries, Aloft radar profiles), but the official Observation.org API is gated, the GBIF copy of waarneming.nl blurs Dutch coordinates to 5 km and lags about two months, and no study reports day-level forecast skill for rarities in north-west Europe; the literature is explicit that raw sighting data mostly encode where birders go. The concrete recommendation is to start now with the **Nutcracker**: the live irruption offers **131 reported locations in eight days**, the species moves slowly and settles in identifiable habitat, and day-level wind is not its driver, so a persistence-plus-habitat model can be built locally in two weekends and scored every day against the real feed on the staging deployment. The **Yellow-browed Warbler** is the right second species for a weather-driven model built over winter and tested next autumn. The first prerequisite is a nightly archive of the sightings table, because production prunes after 15 days and every evaluation depends on that history.

*Reading guide: "verified" means the researchers read the fact on the cited page, API response or repository file on 24 October 2026; "estimate" and "inference" mark the researchers' or this report's own reasoning. Feed numbers for the current week were verified by the coordinator on the live [vogel-radar.nl](https://vogel-radar.nl) feed on the same day (last 7 days, rarity 3 and 4).*

## The crane radar nowcasts one flocking species and does not scale to rarities

The Kraanvogelradar at analytical.sensingclues.org/cranes is, in its authors' words, "a real-time digital twin of the common crane (Grus grus) migration over the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxemburg, and parts of northern France and northwest Germany" (verified: [De Koning 2025, Ecological Informatics 85:102938](https://edepot.wur.nl/683479)). It selects crane observations "reported as 'flying over' (sometimes including direction e.g. 'flying south'), within the study area and seen not more than thirty minutes ago" from the waarneming.nl and observation.org API, propagates each flock with flight algorithms "parametrised using historical GPS tracks and weather reanalysis data", and pulls hourly KNMI wind forecasts four times a day; "it updates its predictions every minute, with a forecast horizon of up to four hours for each crane observation" (verified: [De Koning 2025](https://edepot.wur.nl/683479)). The whole model is an R Shiny app with a separate Python process on the server; the paper names the wind source as the KNMI data platform and the Sensing Clues release post names the **KNMI HARMONIE** model (verified: [De Koning 2025](https://edepot.wur.nl/683479); [Sensing Clues release post](https://www.sensingclues.org/news/crane-migration-radar)). Nature's October 2025 feature puts the effort at about a year and the cost at roughly 15,000 to 20,000 euro within the EU-funded Nature FIRST project, and reports "an average of **100,000 daily visits** during peak migration season" rising to more than 300,000 users a day after national news coverage (verified: [Nature 646:763-765](https://media.nature.com/original/magazine-assets/d41586-025-03314-y/d41586-025-03314-y.pdf)). De Koning told WUR's magazine it "started as a hobby project" (verified: [Resource, 24 October 2024](https://www.resource-online.nl/index.php/2024/10/24/crane-radar-improves-probability-calculation/?lang=en)). The model code is deposited at 4TU.ResearchData as two R files without the API credentials (verified: [4TU record](https://data.4tu.nl/datasets/c51224c6-ad49-46cc-b0aa-abc435c038d8)).

Two properties make the method work and neither applies to rarities. First, cranes are large, diurnal, flocking birds that follow narrow corridors and are reported with a direction, so a new observation can be assimilated: "Whenever the location of a newly observed flock falls within the confidence limits of any flock forecasted by the digital twin ... the model links the new observation to the most likely flock" and re-estimates speed and heading (verified: [De Koning 2025](https://edepot.wur.nl/683479)). Second, the flight model was cross-validated on GPS tracks, and even then "flight speed is the most difficult parameter to forecast" (verified: [De Koning et al. 2024, Ecological Modelling 498:110884](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2024.110884)). A rarity is a single bird that was found sitting somewhere, with no direction, no flock and no tracking-derived behaviour model; the propagation step that is the heart of the crane radar is empty for it. What does transfer, as the researchers inferred, is the plumbing: the ingestion loop, the grid-based probability layer with a four-class plain-language legend ("zeer klein", "klein", "redelijk - opletten!", "groot" on the live page, verified: [analytical.sensingclues.org/cranes](https://analytical.sensingclues.org/cranes/)), the location alert, the one-to-five-minute refresh, and the explicit treatment of two data-quality problems the paper names: observers who report their own position instead of the bird's, and the lag because "many birdwatchers do not immediately report their observations in the field but later back at home" (verified: [De Koning 2025](https://edepot.wur.nl/683479)). One more detail matters for a copycat: the radar's API access "could be accessed through personal connections with one of the founders of the platforms", not through a public developer programme (verified: [De Koning 2025](https://edepot.wur.nl/683479)). A 48-hour crane forecast was announced for autumn 2025 (verified: [Sensing Clues, spring 2025 post](https://sensingclues.org/news/spring-2025-crane-migration)); the February 2026 newsletter does not mention it, so its status is unknown (inference).

"Can this be done for all birds" therefore has a clear answer: not with this method, and nobody in Europe does it with any method. BirdCast forecasts bulk nocturnal migration intensity, not species, from a model "trained on historical NCEP North American Regional Reanalysis (NARR) data and 23 years of bird movements in the atmosphere as detected by the U.S. weather surveillance radar network" (verified: [BirdCast forecast maps](https://birdcast.org/migration-tools/migration-forecast-maps/)). The Dutch FlySafe forecasts serve the Air Force, and a Europe-wide model is "still in development" (verified: [UvA news, June 2026](https://www.uva.nl/en/content/news/news/2026/06/mapping-busy-bird-migration-airspace-together-with-the-air-force-and-the-knmi.html)). EuroBirdPortal shows 567 species as weekly 30 km maps, but it is a viewer: "Third parties request data from the EBP coordinator" (verified: [EBP help](https://eurobirdportal.org/ebp/en/help)). eBird Status and Trends covers 2,980 species on a 3 km grid, but its terms state "No use of eBird Status and Trends Data Products is permitted without the prior written consent of the Cornell Lab of Ornithology" for websites and apps (verified: [eBird S&T terms](https://ebird.org/about/products-access-terms-of-use)). The only all-species real-time system found is Finland's digital twin, fed by 15 million machine-classified audio detections and updated daily on CSC supercomputers (verified abstract: [Ovaskainen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-025-02966-3)). For Vogelradar, "all birds" can only mean an aggregated weekly climatology layer computed from its own data in the EBP or Status and Trends visual shape, with weather conditioning per guild on top. That is a different product from the crane radar, and it is the second product to build, not the first.

## Weather sets the timing of arrivals, observers set the map

The literature on vagrancy is consistent on one point: weather explains when and how many, observers explain where. For Nearctic landbirds in Britain and Ireland, combining Nova Scotia island counts, a Florida tower-kill series and a cold-front "fallout" list gave **R² of 0.79 to 0.93** for the species composition of autumn vagrants (verified abstract: [McLaren et al. 2006, Ibis](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1474-919X.2006.00574.x)). For eastern vagrants, Thorup found numbers "strongly correlated with standard migratory direction, estimated population size and detectability" (verified abstract: [Thorup 2004, Bird Study](https://doi.org/10.1080/00063650409461358)), and Gilroy and Lees concluded that "it is the pattern of observer coverage which determines the number of vagrants discovered" (verified: [Gilroy and Lees 2003](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/vagrancy-theories-are-autumn-vagrants-really-reverse-migrants/)). The Yellow-browed Warbler, the textbook Siberian drift migrant, "might be establishing a new migration route initiated by vagrants", with interannual variation "linked to weather conditions (especially the wind)" (verified excerpts: [Dufour et al. 2022, Movement Ecology](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40462-022-00345-2)); EuroBirdPortal attributed the 2017 influx to high pressure "driving eastern wind directly to NW Europe" (snippet: [EBP blog](https://eurobirdportal.org/ebp/da/blog/EBP-helps-to-understand-movement-patterns-of-rare-species--the-example-of-the-Yellow-browed-Warbler-1488550794/)). The one isolated non-weather driver, geomagnetic disturbance, explains a median of **5.9 percent** of variance in autumn vagrancy across 152 species (verified full text: [Tonelli et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9829733/)). The caveat that runs through all of this is scale: these studies explain between-year and between-species variation, not which site a bird will be found at tomorrow.

Day-ahead skill is proven only for bulk migration and for one rare species with an unusually clean weather signal. BirdCast's models explained up to 81 percent of variation in migration intensity and "62 to 76 percent" when forecasting 1 to 7 days ahead (verified open copy: [Van Doren and Horton 2018](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10092591)). The Dutch North Sea model explained **71 percent** of variance in hourly low-altitude migration and classified more than 80 percent of hours correctly, yet "the percentage of correctly predicted intense migration hours was low" for the top 5 percent (verified: [Bradarić et al. 2024, Dryad](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.8gtht76x0)); rarity arrivals live in exactly that tail. The closest published blueprint for a rarity forecast is the Ross's Gull system in Japan: 16 winters of counts, a self-organising map of sea-level-pressure patterns (sighting probability 0 to 85.7 percent by node) and a regression on backward wind trajectory and date (0 to 95.5 percent), both driven by the operational numerical weather model to give 1 to 5 day daily probabilities; in a two-year follow-up, gulls were seen on **6 of 14 forecast-guided days** (verified full text: [Senzaki et al. 2024, Ecology and Evolution](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11199128/)). Irruptions run on a different clock: Bohemian Waxwing numbers across northern Europe fluctuate in synchrony and relate to Finnish rowan crops (partial r = -0.57 between rowan abundance and arrival timing, n = 29 years), with "most irruptions ... in years with high rowanberry abundance in autumn in northern areas", the opposite of the food-shortage rule (verified full text: [Dale 2023, Journal of Ornithology](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10336-023-02083-7)). Irruption lead time is weeks to months from crop and breeding information; day-level weather is not the driver (inference from Dale 2023 and the Winter Finch Forecast, which publishes no accuracy statistics: [Finch Research Network](https://finchnetwork.org/qa-with-tyler-hoar-compiler-of-the-winter-finch-forecast)).

Three modelling constraints follow. Sample size: "no algorithm predicted consistently well with small sample size (n < 30)" (verified abstract: [Wisz et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1472-4642.2008.00482.x)), and realistic minima are 14 to 25 presence records (verified abstract: [van Proosdij et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.01509)); most Dutch rarity-4 species fall below that per cell-week, so models must pool by guild or use ensembles of small models, which "performed significantly better than standard SDMs. The rarer the species, the more pronounced the effects were" (verified abstract: [Breiner et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12403)). Effort: in the eBird benchmark, "the largest gap among models ... came from adding effort covariates" (verified: [Johnston et al. 2021, bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/574392v2.full-text)), and where effort is unknown, target-group background (all bird records as the biased background) had "as large an effect on predictive performance as the choice of modelling method" (verified abstract: [Phillips et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1890/07-2153.1)). Persistence: apparent stay lengths of vagrants are longer "in more heavily birded regions compared to more sparsely watched ones" (snippet: [Ahmed 2025, British Birds](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/observer-effort-and-its-influence-apparent-stay-lengths-vagrant-north-american)), 45 percent of 1958 to 1965 British rarities were found at weekends (snippet: [British Birds letters](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/letters-165)), and tracked stopover migrants show that **24 percent relocate at least 1 km and 7 percent more than 10 km** within a 20 by 40 km landscape (verified: [Taylor et al. 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0027054)). The honest conclusion is that "where will the next rarity appear" is not something any dataset supports; "which rarities are plausible this week, in which region and habitat, and where are the known ones likely still to be" is, and the BTO already delivers that manually every week from reporting-rate anomalies and the wind outlook (verified: [BTO Migblog, 1 May 2026](https://www.bto.org/our-work/news/blog/migblog/1-may-2026)).

Three consequences shape the design. First, the only exact-coordinate, rarity-labelled, near-real-time Dutch feed is the one Vogelradar already scrapes, and it is the one source whose terms could not be read; the researchers could not find a terms-of-use page, the API documentation requires login, and the unauthenticated per-species endpoint that did answer (returning scientific name, Dutch name, rarity label and "Taxonomy conform AviList", verified: [waarneming.nl species API](https://waarneming.nl/api/v1/species/2/)) had no working list endpoint and no stated stability guarantee. The right move is to apply through the Observation.org FAQ contact form with a clear non-commercial purpose and the crane radar as precedent, because the paper shows Observation International does grant access to such projects (inference from [De Koning 2025](https://edepot.wur.nl/683479)). Second, the licensing posture is fixed by the weakest link: Observation.org on GBIF is CC BY-NC, eBird's API terms are non-commercial with no raw redistribution, Open-Meteo's free tier is non-commercial; the test site must stay free of ads and paid tiers, publish only aggregated cell layers, and attribute every source. Third, the NDFF vulnerable-species policy is on the project's side as long as it stays with vagrants: the policy blurs scarce breeders, raptors, owls and roosts, but "species that occur irregularly in the Netherlands (for example vagrants) are rare but are left out of consideration" because they have no recurring sites (verified PDF: [NDFF Lijst Kwetsbare Soorten v1.2](https://ndff.nl/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Toelichting-Lijst-Kwetsbare-Soorten.pdf)). A habitat model for scarce breeders would undo that blurring; the test site should exclude those species from any habitat tier and show vagrants at cell level only.

## Four model tiers, with persistence and climatology as the bar to beat

The modelling notes lay out a ladder that matches the evidence. Tier 1 is a decayed kernel density over the last 7 days of Vogelradar's own reports: deduplicate reports of one species within about 2 km and 3 days into "individual-sites", weight each site by a damped report count (n^0.3) times an exponential age decay, use a travel-radius bandwidth per species group, and evaluate on H3 cell centroids with scikit-learn's haversine KernelDensity, which supports sample weights and is the canonical species-distribution example (verified: [scikit-learn KernelDensity](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.neighbors.KernelDensity.html); [species KDE example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/neighbors/plot_species_kde.html)). It cannot forecast new arrivals by construction; its honest name is "activity now", and its honest competitor is raw persistence (estimate from the modelling notes: one evening of work, about 10^7 kernel evaluations, under a second in NumPy). Tier 2 multiplies that field by a habitat score: the land-cover class frequencies at a species' historical sites divided by the frequencies at background cells give per-class weights, and the cell score is the dot product with the cell's class fractions from a one-off zonal pass over CORINE with rasterstats (verified: [rasterstats categorical zonal stats](https://pythonhosted.org/rasterstats/manual.html)); MaxEnt via elapid is the principled version if the cheap one shows a backtest gain (verified: [elapid](https://earth-chris.github.io/elapid)). Tier 3 treats wind not as a kernel shift but as a feature, because even GPS-tracked cranes defeat speed forecasts; the directional prior for drift migrants is a hypothesis to test, not an input to assume (inference). Tier 4 is a LightGBM model per guild on H3 resolution 6 cell-days, target "at least one new individual-site in the next 1 or 3 days", features from decayed neighbourhood counts, leave-year-out climatology, habitat fractions, distance to coast, forecast wind, day of year and an effort proxy, with species as a categorical feature so that rarity-4 species borrow strength (tier 5); two years of resolution-6 cell-days is about 7.3 million rows per guild before negative subsampling and about 400,000 after, trainable on a laptop in minutes (estimate; LightGBM parameters verified: [LightGBM docs](https://lightgbm.readthedocs.io/en/stable/Parameters.html)). The upper anchor for skill is the eBird best-practice workflow on a common species with complete checklists: PR-AUC 0.444, MCC 0.390, sensitivity 0.684 (verified: [eBird best practices, encounter rate](https://ebird.github.io/ebird-best-practices/encounter.html)); the researchers' expectation for rarities without effort data is ranking skill driven mostly by climatology and effort proxies, poor calibration, and a new-arrival hit rate that only modestly exceeds climatology (inference).

Evaluation is where the product earns the word "forecast" or does not. The protocol is a rolling-origin daily backtest over archived snapshots: build features only from the table as known on day D, score against reports dated D+1 and D+1 to D+3 from the final revalidated state, which automatically drops the misidentifications that production deletes within a day for rarity 3 and within an hour for rarity 4 (verified: [updater.py](/home/hermen/Documents/projects/vogelradar2.0/updater.py)). Three targets separate the questions: all reports (persistence will win because of stake-outs), first reports of new sites, and new sites more than 10 km from any same-species site in the prior 7 days (the only target where a forecast has to prove itself). The product metric is the top-k hit rate, "share of next-day rare sightings that fall in the top 5 percent of cells", reported next to two baselines: persistence (cells with a same-species report in the last 1 to 3 days plus neighbours) and climatology (historical density for that week of year). For rankings without probabilities the continuous Boyce index is the right measure, because AUC "is prevalence-dependent and hence not an accurate performance index" on presence-only data (verified abstract: [van Proosdij et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.01509); Boyce method: [Hirzel et al. 2006](https://www.unil.ch/biomapper/Download/Hirzel-EcoMod-2006.pdf)); it is about 25 lines of NumPy (estimate). Brier score and log loss apply only to a calibrated tier-4 output, and class balancing "improved discrimination ... but generally hindered calibration" for rare species (verified summary: [Steen et al. 2021](https://methodsblog.com/2022/04/14/spatial-thinning-and-class-balancing-key-choices-lead-to-variation-in-the-performance-of-species-distribution-models-with-citizen-science-data/)), so the map should show percentile classes with sample sizes, never probabilities. No paper reports a top-k hit rate, log-loss or baseline comparison for any rare-bird model, so whatever threshold the developer sets is a design choice, and a published, effort-corrected hit rate for a Dutch rarity model would be a first (inference from the literature notes).

The engineering fits the existing stack and a 1 GB container. H3 resolution 6 (average cell 36.13 km², about 10,000 cells over the Netherlands plus a 200 km buffer) matches the 5 km GBIF generalisation and is the modelling grid; resolution 7 (5.16 km², about 70,000 cells over that extent) is for display of tier-1 output from exact coordinates (verified: [H3 resolution table](https://h3geo.org/docs/core-library/restable/); cell counts are estimates). Per species-day layers are tens of kilobytes: a 300 by 330 pixel PNG with alpha is 10 to 30 KB and 45 species times 7 days is a few megabytes (estimate). Leaflet's built-in image overlay is the lightest renderer, provided the PNG is drawn in Web Mercator pixel space rather than on a lat/lon grid; Leaflet.heat is the wrong tool because it draws points and scales intensity with zoom (verified: [Leaflet.heat](https://github.com/Leaflet/Leaflet.heat); projection point is an inference). The geo stack (rasterio, geopandas, GDAL) stays on the laptop; the container only needs NumPy, h3 and Pillow plus a per-cell feature table of a few hundred kilobytes. A nightly job runs in GitHub Actions (free for a public repository, 2,000 minutes per month for a private one, verified: [GitHub Actions billing](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/managing-billing-for-your-products/about-billing-for-github-actions)) or as a Coolify scheduled task inside the container with the default 300 s timeout (verified: [Coolify scheduled tasks](https://coolify.io/docs/core/automation/scheduled-tasks/overview)). The separate deploy site the developer asked for already exists in outline: the staging application on the dev branch, which scrapes at a longer interval "so the extra instance stays polite towards waarneming.nl" (verified: [api.py](/home/hermen/Documents/projects/vogelradar2.0/api.py)); the forecast tab should read a nightly export of the production table rather than its own scrape, and production stays untouched until a backtest over at least one autumn and one spring is published next to the legend. The marginal cost of all of this is zero as long as the site stays non-commercial (estimate).

Three pitfalls deserve code, not goodwill. Stake-outs: the local snapshot shows one rarity-4 species with 12 reports in 8 days and two with 9 (verified by SQL: [vogelradar.db](/home/hermen/Documents/projects/vogelradar2.0/vogelradar.db)), so reports are not birds, and any model that counts reports will predict car parks and hides; deduplicate to sites, cap site weights, make "first report of a new site" the target. Effort: the same snapshot holds 2,672 rows at rarity 1 and 3,668 at rarity 2 against 358 at rarity 3 and 57 at rarity 4 over eight days (verified by SQL; the snapshot is a stale dev copy and production volumes were not checked), which means the all-species report density per cell is already in the database and can serve as the target-group background without any new data source (inference). Archive: production serves 7 days and prunes after 15 (verified: [database.py](/home/hermen/Documents/projects/vogelradar2.0/database.py)); nothing stores snapshots beyond that, and because revalidation rewrites rows, the archive must keep both the as-known-at-the-time state for features and the final state for targets. Phase 0 is therefore a nightly `archive/{date}.json.gz` of the full sightings table, under 1 MB a day (estimate), started before anything else.

The Nutcracker wins on three grounds. The data are the richest the site has seen for a rarity-class species, and they are still arriving, which turns the coming weeks into a natural experiment that no archived autumn could replace. The mechanism fits the cheap tiers: an irruptive corvid moves at a few tens of kilometres a day and stops where food is, so a two-state site model (stay, or move a short distance) plus a habitat multiplier is a defensible description of the process, whereas for a drift migrant the same model would be a placeholder for a weather term it lacks. And the species sits where the licensing and sensitivity questions are easiest: an irregular visitor is explicitly outside the NDFF blurring list, and nobody needs to be protected from a map of Nutcracker hotspots at hexagon resolution. The weaknesses are known in advance and should be written on the tab: there is no climatology baseline, because irruptions are episodic and the GBIF history for 2024 and 2025 will hold few Nutcracker records (inference), so the bar to beat is persistence; subspecies labels can be revised by validators and the hourly rarity-4 revalidation sweep will rewrite them, so the two feeds should be merged into one species layer with subspecies as an attribute; and the front may fade before the model is polished, which is the argument for exporting the last 15 days tonight and building fast.

The first model, concretely. Data: tonight, export every Notenkraker and Dunsnavelnotenkraker row from the production database (exact coordinates, date, time, count, url, rarity) before pruning removes the first days of the irruption, and start the nightly full-table archive. Units: cluster reports of the species within 2 km and 3 days into sites; the site weight is n_reports^0.3 times exp(-age_days / 5), where the 5-day decay is a guess for a slow mover to be tuned on the first two weeks of archive. Movement: each site contributes a mixture of a "stayed" kernel (Gaussian, 3 to 5 km, matching the stopover studies in which most relocations are within a few kilometres and more than 90 percent of tracked waders stayed within 10 km, verified: [Taylor et al. 2011](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0027054)) with weight p_stay, and a "moved" kernel (exponential or Gaussian tail with a scale of about 30 km and a tail to 100 km, from the 34 to 43 km/day ring-recovery averages) with weight 1 minus p_stay; p_stay starts at 0.8 per day (a prior for a site-bound irruptive, to be fitted on the archive) and the kernel is isotropic in version 1, with a south-westerly drift component estimated from the front's own centroid movement as a version-2 hypothesis for the backtest. Habitat: compute CORINE 2018 class fractions once per H3 resolution 7 cell over the Netherlands (roughly 8,000 cells, estimate from the 5.16 km² average), set version-1 weights by hand from field knowledge (conifer and mixed forest, hazel-rich parks and mature gardens; this is birder knowledge, not a fact verified in the notes), and in version 2 replace them by the class-ratio weights learned from the irruption's own sites against background cells, leave-one-week-out so the habitat score never sees the week it is scored on. Effort: the all-species report count per cell over the last 7 and 30 days from the same database, offered as an "effort-normalised" toggle and used as the background set for the Boyce index. Output: per day, a resolution-7 ranking cut into five percentile classes (top 1, 5, 10, 25 percent, rest), rendered as a PNG in Web Mercator pixel space and shown with Leaflet's image overlay as a new tab on the map page, with the deduplicated sites of the last 3 days drawn beside it as the persistence layer so the eye can compare. The legend should read like the crane radar's, in plain Dutch words rather than numbers, and the tab should say "activiteit nu" with the number of sites behind it and, once available, the hit rates; the visual should feel like a bird map, not an instrument panel.

Evaluation starts on day one. Every morning the job writes the forecast made from the archive as known at 05:00, and every following morning it scores yesterday's forecast against the new sites that appeared: top-5 percent hit rate for all reports, for first reports of new sites, and for new sites more than 10 km from any site in the prior 7 days, each against persistence (cells with a report in the last 3 days plus their ring-1 neighbours) and against the static habitat layer alone, plus the Boyce index of the ranking against the effort background. The log is a CSV from the start, so by the time the irruption ends there is a three-to-five-week record that answers the only question that matters: did the kernel-plus-habitat ranking place new Nutcracker sites in its top 5 percent of cells more often than persistence did. If it did not, the tab stays an "activity now" map and the investment stops at tier 2; if it did, the same harness carries the next species. The schedule is one evening for the export and archive job, one weekend for the tier-1 layer, the local Leaflet tab and the persistence comparison, and a second weekend for the CORINE pass, the effort layer, the backtest script and the deployment to the staging application on the dev branch; production is not touched.

Model two is the Yellow-browed Warbler, and it is where wind enters. Build it over the winter from the GBIF Observation.org history at H3 resolution 6 (the 5 km blur makes finer cells pointless), restricted to September to November, with the Open-Meteo Historical Forecast API supplying ICON-EU wind from 24 November 2022 onward so that three autumns of training see forecast winds rather than smoother reanalysis values (verified availability: [Open-Meteo Historical Forecast API](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-forecast-api)); the target is a new site per cell-day, the features follow the Ross's Gull design (easterly wind component over the previous 48 to 72 hours, a pressure-pattern class, day of year, distance to coast, effort), the model is LightGBM with leave-year-out validation, and the metric is the top-5 percent hit rate for new sites against the leave-year-out climatology. Run it live from September 2027. The Great Grey Shrike is the control to run alongside the Nutcracker now: with 155 reported locations and birds that hold winter territories, the dedupe radius and the stay probability can be tuned where the answer is known, and if the habitat recipe cannot find heath and clear-fells for a shrike it will not find anything for a harder species.

## Conclusion

The crane radar's success is a property of cranes, not of a method that can be pointed at other birds: it needs flocks that are reported in flight with a direction and a flight model trained on tracking data, and none of the 45 rarity-class species that Vogelradar shows in a typical week offers either. The project's real opportunity is different and, in one respect, larger. No published work reports an effort-corrected hit rate for any rarity-occurrence model in north-west Europe, and the ingredients to produce one (a rarity-labelled feed with exact coordinates and a built-in effort proxy, free wind and habitat layers, and a backtest harness that is a few hundred lines of Python) are all in hand. The scientific warning that sighting data mostly map observers is also the product's protection: a tab that shows percentile classes, site counts and its own hit rates next to a persistence baseline cannot overclaim, and if the numbers never beat persistence the "activity now" map is still a feature no alert service offers.

The irruption running outside right now is the reason to start this week rather than after a design phase. A nightly archive begun today is worth more than any model, because every later question (does decay beat raw persistence, does habitat add anything, how long do these birds stay) can only be answered from snapshots that production will otherwise delete in 15 days. Start with the Nutcracker because the process is slow and legible, keep the Yellow-browed Warbler for the winter build when wind can be learned from three archived autumns, and treat the Observation International API request as the one piece of correspondence that decides whether the whole thing rests on a scraper behind a bot check or on a stable feed.
