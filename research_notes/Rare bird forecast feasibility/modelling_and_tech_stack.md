Research date: 2026-10-24. Scope: a hobby-scale test site next to Vogelradar (vogel-radar.nl), production untouched, near-zero cost.

### Inferences
- A rarity-4 species with 12 reports in 8 days (Bronskopeend) is almost certainly one staked-out individual reported by many observers; the data are "reports", not "individuals". Any per-day model must deduplicate reports into individual-site clusters before counting.
- With roughly 45 rare species visible per week and only a handful of reports each, per-species supervised models (tier 4) are not feasible from the 7-day store alone; they need the GBIF or eBird history (below), and even then most rarity-4 species need pooling (tier 5).
- The daily revalidation deletions mean a training archive must store daily snapshots as-known-at-the-time (for backtest inputs) and the final revalidated state (for targets). Nothing in the repo currently archives snapshots beyond 15 days; a tiny nightly export job is the first prerequisite for any evaluation.

## Key question 1: Model tiers, simplest first (algorithms, libraries, data needs, skill expectations)

### Takeaway
Tier 1 (decayed, species-group-radius KDE on the last 7 days) is a weekend job on NumPy or scikit-learn and will beat nothing but "no map"; its honest competitor is persistence. Tiers 2 and 3 add cheap priors (habitat similarity from a land-cover raster, wind-shifted kernels) that are plausible but unproven for vagrants. Tier 4 (LightGBM on an H3 or km grid trained on 1 to 2 years of GBIF/eBird history with effort correction) is the only tier with a real chance of forecasting new arrivals, and it must be pooled by guild (tier 5) because rarity-4 species have too few records.

### Cited Findings
Tier 1, kernel density with decay and travel radius:
- scikit-learn 1.9.1 `KernelDensity` supports kernels gaussian, tophat, epanechnikov, exponential, linear, cosine; `bandwidth` can be a float or 'scott' or 'silverman'; `fit(X, sample_weight=...)` is supported (since 0.20), `score_samples` returns log density; the doc notes density normalisation is only correct for Euclidean distance. Verified. [scikit-learn KernelDensity](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.neighbors.KernelDensity.html)
- The official scikit-learn species-distribution example fits `KernelDensity(bandwidth=0.04, metric="haversine", kernel="gaussian", algorithm="ball_tree")` on lat/lon converted to radians, evaluates on a land-masked grid and uses `np.exp(score_samples)`. Verified. [scikit-learn species KDE example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/neighbors/plot_species_kde.html)
- `sample_weight` lets temporal decay (for example weight = exp(-age_days / tau)) and per-report deduplication weights be applied without custom code. Verified from the same page.

### Inferences
- Tier 1 concrete recipe (estimate of effort: one evening): cluster reports of the same species within 2 km and 3 days into "individual-sites"; weight each site w = n_reports^0.3 (damps stake-outs) times exp(-age_days / tau) with tau per rarity (rarity 4: 3 days, rarity 3: 5 days; these taus are guesses to be tuned by backtest); bandwidth per species group (sedentary passerine 5 km, waterbird 15 km, raptor or seabird 30 km; guesses); evaluate on H3 res 6 or res 7 cell centroids with haversine KDE; normalise per species to percentiles. Cost: 70,000 cells times a few hundred points is about 10^7 kernel evaluations, well under a second in NumPy (estimate).
- Tier 1 cannot forecast new arrivals by construction; it is a smoothed persistence map. Its only honest claim is "where rarities are being seen now, smoothed for travel", which is still a useful UI feature. The backtest will show whether decay and radius beat raw persistence at all (estimate: marginally, for mobile species; not for staked-out passerines).
- Tier 2 cheapest variant: for each species (or guild) compute the land-cover class frequency vector at historical GBIF sightings (within the 5 km generalisation) and at background cells; the ratio per class gives a per-class weight; the per-cell habitat score is the dot product of the cell's class fractions and those weights. This is a Bayes-factor style weighting, needs no fitting library, and runs in seconds. MaxEnt via elapid is the principled version but adds the glmnet or sklearn solver dependency and the background-sampling design; use it only when tier 2 shows any backtest gain.
- Tier 2 limitation that follows from the GBIF finding: Observation.org coordinates on GBIF are snapped to 0.05 degrees (about 3.4 km east-west by 5.6 km north-south at 52 N, estimate), so habitat fractions must be computed at the same coarse scale (H3 res 6 or 5 km cells) or the habitat signal will be noise. Fine-scale habitat (res 7, 10 m WorldCover) only makes sense with the Vogelradar archive's own exact coordinates, which will take a year to accumulate.
- Tier 3 is best treated as a feature for tier 4 (wind components u and v at 10 m and 80 m or 120 m, averaged over the last 24 to 48 h and the next 24 h) rather than as a kernel shift. A kernel shift needs a species-specific displacement model; the crane paper shows even GPS-tracked cranes with known flight behaviour give speed errors that are hard to forecast. For vagrants, the directional prior per season (for example south-west in autumn for Siberian passerines, easterly winds raising expected arrivals on the coast) is a hypothesis to test in the backtest, not an input to assume.
- Tier 4 design: unit = cell-day (H3 res 6, about 10,000 cells, see question 3) times species-group; target = at least one new individual-site reported in the next 1 or 3 days; features = decayed counts of the same species and the guild within 1, 2 and 3 rings of cells over 1, 3, 7 and 30 days, climatology (historical rate per week of year from GBIF, computed leave-year-out), habitat fractions, distance to coast, wind, day-of-year (as sin and cos), observer-effort proxies (count of all-species reports in the cell over the last 30 days as a target-group background analogue), day-of-week. Model = one LightGBM binary or poisson model per guild with species as a categorical feature (pooling, tier 5). Negative sampling: keep all positives and 2 to 5% of negatives with `init_score` or weights to correct the base rate (estimate: 2 years of res-6 cell-days is 7.3 million rows per guild before sampling; after 5% negative sampling about 400,000 rows, trainable on a laptop in minutes).
- Realistic skill (inference, no direct source for rare-bird forecasting exists in what I found): the eBird book's own test metrics for a common species with complete checklists (PR-AUC 0.44, MCC 0.39) are an upper anchor; for rarities without effort data, expect ranking skill (AUC against background 0.75 to 0.85 driven mostly by climatology and effort proxies) but poor calibration and a top-5%-cells hit rate for genuinely new arrivals that may only modestly exceed climatology. Persistence will dominate same-individual re-sightings. The first milestone to aim for is "beats climatology for new-arrival hit rate at the 5% cell budget on a 3-month holdout"; if it does not, stop at tier 2 and present the map as "activity now", not a forecast.
- GeoLifeCLEF's lesson that single-positive-label training biases multi-label evaluation applies directly: training a "cell contains species X" model on presence-only reports without effort correction learns observer behaviour. Target-group background (all bird reports as background) and the eBird-style standardised effort are the two cheap corrections available here.

## Key question 2: Evaluation design (backtesting, metrics, baselines, leakage, uncertainty)

### Takeaway
Use a rolling-origin daily backtest over archived snapshots with three target definitions (all reports, first reports of new individual-sites, and new sites more than 10 km from any same-species site in the prior 7 days), report top-k cell hit rate and PR-AUC against persistence and climatology, and show on the map only percentile classes with sample sizes, never probabilities.

### Cited Findings
- scikit-learn 1.9.1 `TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits, max_train_size, test_size, gap)` gives forward-chaining splits where each training set is a superset of the previous one; `gap` excludes samples between train and test (useful to drop the day being forecast from the features). Verified. [TimeSeriesSplit](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.TimeSeriesSplit.html)
- scikit-learn metrics: `average_precision_score` summarises the precision-recall curve (random predictions score the positive fraction); `roc_auc_score`; `top_k_accuracy_score`; `log_loss`; `brier_score_loss` is the squared error between predicted probabilities and binary outcomes (the page excerpt mentions it; the formula section was beyond the fetched portion). Verified in part. [scikit-learn model evaluation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/model_evaluation.html)
- Continuous Boyce index: bins predictions into moving windows, computes the predicted-to-expected ratio of presences per window against background area, and takes the Spearman rank correlation of P/E against window value; R implementations exist in ecospat (`ecospat.boyce`, whose maintainers suggest Kendall or Pearson may be more informative), enmSdmX (`evalContBoyce`), spatstat.explore (`boyce`), modEvA and itsdm (`evaluate_po`); it needs presences plus background. No dedicated Python package was found in two searches. Verified. [ecospat.boyce](https://rdrr.io/pkg/ecospat/man/ecospat.boyce.html), [enmSdmX evalContBoyce](https://rdrr.io/cran/enmSdmX/man/evalContBoyce.html), [spatstat boyce](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/spatstat.explore/versions/3.8-2/topics/boyce), [itsdm evaluate_po](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/itsdm/html/evaluate_po.html), [r-bloggers model evaluation with presence points](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2022/05/model-evaluation-with-presence-points-and-raster-predictions/)
- Randin et al. 2006 (continuous Boyce) found on 114 plant species that Boyce indices are closer to AUC than to MaxKappa and fairly insensitive to prevalence; a 2023 preprint notes the standard estimator "relies upon having large number of presences", which matters for rare species. Verified via search summaries. [Hirzel et al. 2006 PDF](https://www.unil.ch/biomapper/Download/Hirzel-EcoMod-2006.pdf), [Smoothing the Boyce index preprint](https://www.authorea.com/doi/full/10.22541/au.169941725.58752741/v1)
- elapid provides `GeographicKFold` and `checkerboard_split` for spatially clustered cross-validation. Verified. [elapid](https://earth-chris.github.io/elapid)
- The eBird book evaluates on a held-out 20% that receives the same spatiotemporal subsampling as the training data, and selects thresholds on the MCC-F1 curve. Verified. [Encounter rate chapter](https://ebird.github.io/ebird-best-practices/encounter.html)
- The Crane Radar page makes no accuracy or validation statement; the underlying paper validated with cross-validation on GPS tracks, not on sightings. Verified. [Crane Radar](https://sensingclues.org/craneradar), [De Koning et al. 2024](https://dare.uva.nl/id/1d5c9eb8-8cd5-49b1-9fe7-aa73798fc907)
- Production deletes rows that disappear on waarneming.nl (misidentifications) within a day for rarity 3 and within an hour for rarity 4. Verified. [updater.py](/home/hermen/Documents/projects/vogelradar2.0/updater.py)

### Inferences
- Backtest protocol (no library needed beyond pandas): for each origin day D in the holdout, build features only from the snapshot as it was known at D (the nightly archive), produce a ranking of cells per species or guild, and score against reports dated D+1 (and D+1 to D+3) from the final revalidated archive. This separates "what the model could know" from "what later turned out to be true" and automatically excludes deleted misidentifications from targets.
- Top-k hit rate is the metric that matches the product: "share of next-day rare sightings that fall in the top 5% of cells". Compute it per guild and pooled, and always next to the two baselines: persistence (top cells = cells with a same-species report in the last 1 to 3 days, padded with neighbours) and climatology (historical GBIF density for that week of year). Expect persistence to win the "all reports" target by a wide margin because of stake-outs; the "new site more than 10 km away" target is where a forecast has to prove itself.
- Boyce index in Python is about 25 lines with NumPy: sort cells by predicted score, slide a window of width 10% of the score range in steps of 1%, compute (presences in window / all presences) divided by (cells in window / all cells), then `scipy.stats.spearmanr` of P/E against window midpoint. It is the right metric for tiers 1 to 3 (no probabilities, only rankings).
- Brier score and log loss are only meaningful for tier 4 outputs after calibration (isotonic or a monotone GAM as in the eBird book) and against a defined absence: use "cell-day with any bird report but not this species" as the pseudo-absence to keep effort comparable, and report that the absolute values are not comparable with literature values.
- Leakage list to enforce in code: (1) stake-outs: deduplicate to individual-sites before both features and targets; (2) revalidation deletions: targets from the final state, features from the as-known state; (3) climatology computed leave-year-out; (4) coordinate generalisation in GBIF: never mix exact Vogelradar coordinates and 0.05 degree GBIF coordinates at res 7 without noting it; (5) weather: use the Historical Forecast API for training wind so that training and inference see forecast values, not reanalysis (the ERA5 archive has a 5-day delay and is smoother); (6) day-of-week and holiday effort effects: include as features or expect weekend spikes to be learned as "habitat".
- Uncertainty on the map: show per species or guild five percentile classes (top 1%, 5%, 10%, 25%, rest) of the ranking, plus a small label "based on N sites in the last 7 days" and "backtest: X% of new sightings fell in the top 5% of cells (persistence: Y%, climatology: Z%)". Hide species layers with fewer than 3 sites. Rankings avoid implying probabilities the model cannot calibrate.

### Gaps
- No source gives a benchmark for top-k hit rates on rare bird reports; the thresholds proposed are design choices.
- The `brier_score_loss` formula text was outside the fetched part of the scikit-learn page (the first 100,000 characters); the definition used here is from general knowledge.

## Key question 3: Spatial engineering (grid, extent, raster tooling, storage, file sizes)

### Takeaway
H3 resolution 6 (about 36 km2 per cell, about 10,000 cells over NL plus a 200 km buffer) is the right default for modelling given GBIF's 5 km coordinate generalisation; resolution 7 (about 5 km2, about 70,000 cells) is for display of tier 1 output from exact Vogelradar coordinates. Per species-day layers are tens of KB as uint8 or as a sparse top-cells list; 45 rare species times 7 days is a few MB, not hundreds.

### Cited Findings
- H3 resolution table: res 5 average area 252.90 km2, edge 9.85 km, 2,016,842 cells; res 6: 36.13 km2, edge 3.72 km, 14,117,882 cells; res 7: 5.16 km2, edge 1.41 km (extrapolated), 98,825,162 cells; res 8: 0.74 km2, edge 0.53 km. Verified. [H3 resolution table](https://h3geo.org/docs/core-library/restable/)

For 300 species (the whole universe, if ever) multiply by about 7: still under 200 MB dense, tens of MB compressed. The geo stack (rasterio, geopandas, shapely) should stay off the VPS: do zonal stats once on the laptop and ship a small per-cell feature table (10,000 cells times about 20 features float16 is 400 KB).

## Key question 6: Pitfalls (observer bias, duplicates, rarity drift, sensitive species, licensing, overclaiming)

### Takeaway
The dominant failure mode is learning observer behaviour (stake-outs, weekends, hotspots) and calling it bird ecology. Licensing is the second: waarneming.nl's own terms could not be retrieved, its GBIF copy is CC BY-NC with 5 km coordinate blurring, and eBird forbids redistribution and asks for derived products to be submitted. The NDFF vulnerable-species policy explicitly excludes vagrants from blurring but blurs scarce breeders, raptors, owls and roosts, which is exactly the set a habitat model would otherwise locate.

### Cited Findings
- Observer bias and effort: Phillips et al. 2009 showed occurrence collection is spatially biased toward easily accessed areas and that background should carry the same bias (target-group background); Fithian et al. note TGB cannot separate inaccessible areas from accessible areas where all species are rare and does not correct within-accessible-region bias. Verified via search summaries. [Phillips 2009](https://www.whoi.edu/cms/files/Phillips_EcolApp_2009_53454.pdf), [Fithian et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1403.7274)
- The eBird book corrects effort by including duration, distance, speed, observers, start time and date as predictors, by spatiotemporal subsampling, and by predicting at a standardised effort. Verified. [Encounter rate chapter](https://ebird.github.io/ebird-best-practices/encounter.html)
- Duplicate reports: the local DB shows a single rarity-4 species with 12 reports in 8 days (Bronskopeend) and two others with 9. Verified. [vogelradar.db](/home/hermen/Documents/projects/vogelradar2.0/vogelradar.db)
- Rarity classes: Vogelradar maps both "zeldzaam" and "vrij zeldzaam" to 3, so the stored integer already merges two waarneming.nl classes; the revalidation sweep syncs rarity corrections, so a row's rarity can change after first scrape. Verified. [scraper.py](/home/hermen/Documents/projects/vogelradar2.0/scraper.py), [updater.py](/home/hermen/Documents/projects/vogelradar2.0/updater.py)
- Sensitive species (NDFF "Toelichting Lijst Kwetsbare Soorten", version 1.2, last updated 29 May 2026): data are open in principle unless a species is threatened, its habitat vulnerable or disturbance likely; spatial blurring to 1 x 1 km, 5 x 5 km or 10 x 10 km squares depending on how easily a location can be inferred, with the option of hiding entirely for species with only a few populations; temporal blurring (delayed publication for weeks, or only during a sensitive period) as an alternative. For birds: species sensitive to disturbance or persecution qualify (scarce breeders, colony breeders, communal roosts, raptors, owls, finches and buntings at winter concentrations; examples named: Bijeneter, Hop, woodpeckers, Ransuil roosts, Steltkluut, Griel, Strandplevier), while "species that occur irregularly in the Netherlands (for example vagrants) are rare but are left out of consideration" because they have no specific recurring sites. The document also notes that mobile species may not need blurring because a sighting says little about where the animal is next. Verified (PDF text extracted). [NDFF Lijst Kwetsbare Soorten](https://ndff.nl/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Toelichting-Lijst-Kwetsbare-Soorten.pdf)
- waarneming.nl API documentation exists at waarneming.nl/api/docs (REST, OAuth2, observations, species, locations, exports) but the page returned HTTP 403 to the fetcher and no licence or terms page was found in two searches; Observation International's site says only that it provides "a worldwide platform for storing, validating and sharing nature information". Verified (negative result). [waarneming.nl API docs](https://waarneming.nl/api/docs/), [Observation International](https://observation-international.org/en/)
- Observation.org's GBIF dataset: CC BY-NC 4.0, coordinates generalised to 0.05 degrees (floor(coord * 20) / 20), uncertainty 5,000 m. Verified. [GBIF dataset 8a863029](https://api.gbif.org/v1/dataset/8a863029-f435-446a-821e-275f4f641165)
- GBIF data use: downloads come as Simple, Darwin Core Archive or Species List via website, API, rgbif or pygbif; identical download queries within 48 hours return the previous download; "users who download data agree to cite a DOI". Verified. [GBIF data use](https://techdocs.gbif.org/en/data-use/)
- eBird terms (support article): "All data are free to use for any non-commercial purposes related to basic and applied research and education"; commercial use needs explicit permission; data cannot be redistributed in original format or passed to another user; "If you use eBird data to create derived products, an electronic copy of the product should be submitted to eBird"; citation required; data are downloadable after a request at ebird.org/data/download and periodically uploaded to GBIF. Verified. [eBird data terms support article](https://support.ebird.org/support/solutions/articles/48001078113)
- eBird Status and Trends products have separate terms: redistribution through websites, APIs or software tools needs prior written permission. Verified via search summary. [Status and Trends terms](https://ebird.org/about/products-access-terms-of-use)
- WorldCover is CC BY 4.0 with the attribution line "(c) ESA WorldCover project 2021 / Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data (2021) processed by ESA WorldCover consortium". Verified. [WorldCover data access](https://esa-worldcover.org/en/data-access)
- Open-Meteo data are CC BY 4.0 and the free tier is non-commercial only. Verified. [Open-Meteo pricing](https://open-meteo.com/en/pricing)

### Inferences
- Stake-out bias is the single largest confound: it inflates persistence, makes "reports" a poor proxy for "birds", and will make any model that counts reports predict car parks and hides. Mitigations: dedupe to sites, cap the per-site weight (log or power), model "first report of a new site" as the target, and use all-species report density as an effort covariate.
- Revalidation deletions cut both ways: deleted misidentifications should not be targets, but a model trained only on surviving reports is slightly biased toward "easy" rarities; accept this and note it.
- Rarity drift: waarneming.nl's class per species can change seasonally or over years (a species common in winter may be flagged rare in summer); because the training history from GBIF has no rarity field, define the species set for the test from Vogelradar's own archive (species that reached rarity 3 or 4 in the last 12 months) and keep the per-species rarity history to see drift. The merged "zeldzaam" and "vrij zeldzaam" class means the archive cannot distinguish them retroactively; if that matters, change the scraper mapping in a separate change, not as part of this test.
- Sensitive species: a habitat-weighted model of scarce breeders (raptors, owls, Bijeneter, Steltkluut) would effectively reverse the NDFF blurring. Restrict the test site to species classes the NDFF document explicitly leaves open (vagrants and irregular visitors) and exclude any species on the LKS from habitat tiers; show vagrants at cell resolution only (res 6 or 7), never at point level, and add the NDFF rationale to the about page.
- Licensing posture for a non-commercial test site: GBIF (Observation.org CC BY-NC plus eBird CC BY) is usable for climatology and habitat priors with DOI citation; eBird's own EBD adds the "submit derived products" obligation and the no-redistribution rule, so prefer the GBIF copies and do not publish raw points, only aggregated cell layers. For the live waarneming.nl scrape, the test site inherits whatever understanding Vogelradar already operates under; the safest framing is aggregated, non-commercial, attributed, and ask office@observation.org before anything public that goes beyond what production already shows.
- Overclaiming: use the words "activity now" for tiers 1 to 3 and "expected" only for a tier-4 layer that has beaten climatology in the published backtest; show the backtest numbers next to the legend; never show a probability without a calibration plot; keep the production app free of the layer until it has survived at least one autumn and one spring.

- waarneming.nl terms of use and API licence: not retrieved (403 for the API docs; no terms page surfaced in searches). This must be checked by hand or by email before a public test site.
- Cornell Lab's "eBird Data Access Terms of Use" page returned 403; the support article and search summaries disagree on whether derived products may be redistributed at all, so treat that as unresolved.

## Key question 7: Worked examples and prior art

### Takeaway
The eBird Best Practices book is the only end-to-end worked example that maps onto this problem (R, not Python, but every step translates to pandas and LightGBM). The Crane Radar is the closest Dutch precedent and combines waarneming.nl reports, weather and 20,000 historic sightings for a single species with known behaviour. No bird-specific H3 or deck.gl heatmap blog post surfaced; only generic deck.gl H3 examples.

### Cited Findings
- eBird Best Practices (version 2.0, 2023): full R workflow from data filtering through encounter-rate and relative-abundance models with calibration and evaluation, see question 1 for specifics. Verified. [eBird Best Practices](https://ebird.github.io/ebird-best-practices/), [Encounter rate](https://ebird.github.io/ebird-best-practices/encounter.html)
- Crane Migration Radar (Wageningen University & Research and Sensing Clues Foundation, part of the Horizon Europe project Nature FIRST, initiative of Dr. Koen de Koning): a real-time map of crane sightings and expected movements over the next few hours; combines birder observations from Waarneming.nl in NL, BE and DE with current weather data, over 20,000 historic sightings and knowledge of crane biology; positions update every five minutes; no accuracy statement on the page. Verified. [Crane Radar](https://sensingclues.org/craneradar), [WUR news](https://www.wur.nl/en/news/follow-crane-migration-live-crane-radar)
- Sensing Clues' analysis of GPS-tracked cranes found a strong link between departure and wind speed, wind direction, precipitation, cloud cover and temperature, and the model uses wind predictions to refine where a group is likely to pass. Verified via search summary of the Sensing Clues news tag. [Sensing Clues predictive analytics](https://sensingclues.org/news/tag/predictive+analytics)
- De Koning et al. 2024: three species-specific individual-based models at kilometre and 15-minute to hour resolution, cross-validated on GPS tracks; recorded speed and direction improve forecasts under 2 hours; wind at flight altitude improves all ranges. Verified. [UvA DARE record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/1d5c9eb8-8cd5-49b1-9fe7-aa73798fc907)
- GeoLifeCLEF 2023 and 2025 overviews: continental-scale species composition prediction from about 5 million presence-only records evaluated on standardised presence-absence plots; 2025 added a geographically shifted test set with many rare species. Verified (abstracts). [arXiv 2509.25816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25816), [HAL hal-05319448](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-05319448v1)
- deck.gl H3HexagonLayer gallery example (pydeck) and a marimo hexagon-binning notebook exist as generic templates; no bird-specific H3 or deck.gl write-up was found. Verified (negative result). [pydeck H3 example](https://deckgl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/gallery/h3_hexagon_layer.html), [marimo hexagon heatmap](https://marimo.io/gallery/l/deck-gl-hexagon-heatmap)

- The Crane Radar's design (one well-studied migratory species, GPS-derived behavioural parameters, wind at flight altitude, hours-ahead horizon) is not transferable to multi-species vagrant forecasting; what is transferable is the data plumbing (waarneming.nl reports plus Open-Meteo-style wind, frequent refresh) and the framing as "expected movement" rather than "probability".
- The eBird book's structure is the right skeleton for the tier-4 notebook: filter, subsample, train with effort covariates, calibrate, evaluate on a holdout with explicit baselines, predict at standardised effort.

## Phased recommendation (synthesis)

### Takeaway
Phase 0 (one evening): start archiving nightly snapshots now, because every later phase needs them. Phase 1 (one weekend): tier-1 decayed KDE on H3 res 7, PNG overlays, a separate static test site, persistence baseline shown next to it. Phase 2 (second weekend): backtest harness over the archive with top-5% hit rate and Boyce, plus CORINE-based habitat weights at res 6 from GBIF history. Phase 3 (several weekends, after an autumn of archive): pooled LightGBM per guild at res 6 with wind, climatology and effort proxies; promote to "expected" only if it beats climatology on new-site hit rate.

### Cited Findings
- All inputs are available at zero cost under non-commercial terms: H3 (open), CORINE and WorldCover (open, CC BY for WorldCover), GBIF (CC BY and CC BY-NC datasets with DOI citation), Open-Meteo (CC BY 4.0, 10,000 calls per day free), GitHub Actions (free for public repos), Coolify scheduled tasks (built in). Verified; see sources in questions 3, 5 and 6.
- Compute and memory needs for tiers 1 to 3 fit a 1 GB VPS and all tiers fit a laptop or a free Actions runner. Estimate; see question 3.

### Inferences
- Phase 0 deliverable: a nightly job that writes `archive/{date}.json.gz` with the full `sightings` table as known at that moment (about 7,000 rows, under 1 MB, estimate) to a volume or a private repo; nothing else changes.
- Phase 1 deliverables: `build_layers.py` (NumPy, h3, Pillow, no geo stack) producing per-species and per-guild PNGs in EPSG:3857 pixel space plus `index.json`; a one-file Leaflet page on a test subdomain with species toggle, 7-day slider, and a "yesterday's sightings" persistence layer for visual comparison; about page stating "smoothed recent activity, not a forecast" and the NDFF rationale for cell-level display.
- Phase 2 deliverables: `backtest.py` with rolling-origin evaluation over the archive, three target definitions, top-k hit rate, PR-AUC, Boyce, and persistence and climatology baselines; `habitat.py` doing a one-off zonal pass over CORINE 100 m for H3 res 6 cells and the class-ratio weights from GBIF 2023 to 2025 occurrences; tier-1 parameters (tau, bandwidth per guild) tuned on the backtest; the test site gains a "habitat-weighted" toggle and shows the backtest numbers in the legend.
- Phase 3 deliverables: `train.py` on the laptop or Actions (LightGBM, guild-pooled, species as categorical, negative subsampling, Historical Forecast API wind backfill), `predict.py` small enough to run as a Coolify scheduled task, calibration plot, and a published backtest page; the "expected" wording is unlocked by a documented win over climatology.
- Stop rules: if phase 2 shows tier 1 does not beat persistence and habitat weighting does not beat tier 1 on the new-site target, keep the product as an "activity now" map and do not invest in phase 3.
