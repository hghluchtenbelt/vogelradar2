# Scientific literature: predictability of rare and vagrant bird occurrence, and occurrence modelling with citizen-science bird data

Scope note. Compiled 2026-10-24 for a hobby developer in the Netherlands who wants to build a test "rare bird occurrence heatmap" for the Netherlands and neighbouring countries. Sources were located through web search, Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar and Europe PMC; where only an abstract or a press summary could be read, this is stated. "Verified" below means the statement was read in the primary source (abstract, full text, or official data repository page). "Snippet" means it comes from a search-engine summary of a page that could not be opened (several British Birds and BirdGuides pages block automated access). "Inference" means my own reading of the evidence. No em-dashes are used in this file.

## Key question 1: Drivers of vagrancy and rarity occurrence, and how strongly weather predicts arrivals

### Takeaway
Weather is a well-documented driver of the timing of vagrant arrivals (Atlantic depressions for Nearctic landbirds, anticyclones with easterly flow for Siberian passerines, warm southerly anticyclonic flow for spring overshoots), but the quantified studies explain inter-annual or between-species variation in numbers rather than day-level arrivals at specific sites. Population size, migratory direction and observer coverage come out as strong or dominant predictors wherever they are tested, and the one study that isolates a non-weather driver (geomagnetic disturbance) explains only about 6 percent of variance.

### Cited Findings

Nearctic vagrants in western Europe
- McLaren, Lees, Field and Collins (2006, Ibis 148: 707-726) regressed autumn frequencies of Nearctic landbird species in Britain and Ireland (1956-2003) on counts from Nova Scotia islands (Sable, Seal; 1963-2002), a Florida TV-tower kill series (1955-1980) and a Nova Scotia "Fallout" list from 11 October 1998 of birds believed to have followed a cold front offshore into strong south-west flow. Species traits were body mass, wing length, SD of mass as a lipid-capacity proxy, a five-level migratory span index, breeding latitude, distance to normal range, day versus night migration, and a population-change index. The Fallout list combined with the island and Florida counts gave R2 = 0.79 to 0.93; October counts of larger, longer-distance migrants best represented the species reaching Britain and Ireland; geographic remoteness had a negative effect; population change was important; the authors conclude many Nearctic vagrants originate in flights off the south-east USA. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1474-919X.2006.00574.x)
- Elkins (1979, British Birds) linked North American landbird arrivals in Britain and Ireland in 1967-1976 to fast-moving warm-sector depressions crossing the North Atlantic; later British Birds reviews covered 1987-1996 and 1997-2006 in the same meteorological framing. [snippet] [Source](https://www.britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/nearctic-landbirds-britain-and-ireland-meteorological-analysis); [Source](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/further-thoughts-transatlantic-vagrancy-landbirds-britain-ireland)
- A BirdGuides article on "predicting Nearctic passerines in autumn" discusses the Elkins (1979) and Lees and Gilroy (2009) weather models and states that forecasting Nearctic arrivals "involves more nuances and less reliability than predicting Palearctic arrivals". [snippet; page blocks automated access] [Source](https://www.birdguides.com/articles/migration/predicting-nearctic-passerines-in-autumn/)
- Lees, Farnsworth and Dufour (2026, Current Biology, "Storms, budworms and vagrant birds in a changing world") discuss the environmental drivers behind "an unprecedented arrival of vagrant North American songbirds in Western Europe in September 2023", framing displacement as routinely influenced by atmospheric and ecological connections (the title points to storms and spruce budworm outbreaks as drivers). [verified: abstract only; body not read] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2026.02.057)
- Zawadzki, Veit and Manne (2019, Ardea 107) analysed vagrant Ash-throated Flycatchers on the North American east coast: vagrancy was "predominantly explained by the growing breeding population size" (BBS index), with additional significant effects of annual production of young (MAPS) and three measures of air circulation from the NCEP North American Regional Reanalysis; "years of high reproductive success bring larger numbers ... and this number is enhanced when weather conditions are especially favourable". [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.5253/arde.v107i2.a2)

Siberian and eastern vagrants in western Europe
- Thorup (2004, Bird Study 51) tested reverse migration with multiple regression on numbers of reverse migrants recorded in north-west European countries: numbers were "strongly correlated with standard migratory direction, estimated population size and detectability", distance from breeding areas was not supported, and species that migrate eastward in autumn (mostly eastern European breeders) reverse-migrate more consistently than southward migrants. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00063650409461358)
- Gilroy and Lees (2003, British Birds 96: 427-438) argue autumn vagrancy is not confined to a 180-degree "reverse migration shadow", that vagrancy occurs in all directions, and that "it is the pattern of observer coverage which determines the number of vagrants discovered"; they suggest some regular vagrants may be making annual migrations to undiscovered wintering grounds in western Europe or West Africa. [verified: repository abstract] [Source](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/vagrancy-theories-are-autumn-vagrants-really-reverse-migrants/)
- Bozó and Csörgő (2023, Ibis, "Causes of vagrancy of North Asian passerines in western Europe") review explanations split into weather factors versus an internal (orientation) defect, plus climate-driven range expansion after population increase; they conclude that "weather, habitat use, feeding habit, breeding success and climate change are important factors ... together with the number of observers playing an important role in contributing data". [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/ibi.13226)
- Dufour et al. (2022, Movement Ecology 10) use the Yellow-browed Warbler as a model: the species "is still mostly a vagrant in Western Europe but might be establishing a new migration route initiated by vagrants"; interannual variation in abundance "could also be linked to weather conditions (especially the wind) which may block or facilitate their arrival in Western Europe or by short variations of the Earth's magnetic field"; Iberian records were under 1 percent of UK records and under 8 percent of Swedish and French records, with a 2 to 3 fold increase in Iberia over 10 years. [verified: full text excerpts] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40462-022-00345-2)
- Dufour et al. (2021, Current Biology 31, "A new westward migration route in an Asian passerine bird") is the primary tracking and isotope paper behind that claim. [title and year verified via Crossref; abstract not retrieved] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2021.09.086)
- EuroBirdPortal attributed the autumn 2017 Yellow-browed Warbler influx to high-pressure systems "driving eastern wind directly to NW Europe"; thousands now occur each autumn on a front from Scandinavia to Iberia. [snippet] [Source](https://eurobirdportal.org/ebp/da/blog/EBP-helps-to-understand-movement-patterns-of-rare-species--the-example-of-the-Yellow-browed-Warbler-1488550794/)
- Siberian Accentor 2016: British Birds reports about 231 individuals in Europe in autumn and winter 2016/17 against no more than five per year previously, alongside more than 7,000 Yellow-browed Warblers in Britain in September to October 2016; a companion British Birds paper argues the influx "resulted from an unusual conjunction of meteorological factors" while discussing other mechanisms. [snippet] [Source](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/siberian-accentors-europe-autumn-2016-and-first-british-records); [Source](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/what-brought-about-influx-siberian-accentors-europe-autumn-2016-contribution-debate); BirdGuides counted 59 outside Russia in 13 days (4 to 16 October 2016). [snippet] [Source](https://birdguides.com/articles/an-exceptional-influx-of-siberian-accentors-to-northern-europe)
- The 1988 eastern-vagrant autumn in Britain was linked to "an intense anticyclone which extended much farther west into Europe than normal" creating a strong mean easterly airflow on its southern flank. [snippet] [Source](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/eastern-vagrants-britain-autumn-1988)
- A 2018 Avian Research study of ringing data from Far East Russia found that for Pallas's Leaf Warbler, autumn wind speed had a significant negative effect on migration (departure side, not European arrival). [snippet] [Source](https://doaj.org/article/8ea754616729458ca201309c469dfd9a)

Spring overshoots
- Birding sources describe the overshoot setup as a high-pressure ridge from North Africa to the Channel with clear skies, warm temperatures and light tailwinds, with airflow arcing from east to south to south-west around Iberia so that most birds land in south-west Britain (late March 2002 example). BTO reported an "astonishing arrival" of 20-plus Golden Orioles in mid-April 2024 as southerlies strengthened. No formal analytical study of overshoots was found. [snippet] [Source](https://www.birdguides.com/articles/iberian-rarity-bonanza-late-march-2002); [Source](https://ociweb.bto.org/our-work/news/blog/migblog/migblog-19-04-24)

Geomagnetic disturbance as a non-weather driver
- Tonelli, Youngflesh and Tingley (2023, Scientific Reports) used 2,226,936 USGS banding captures of 152 landbird species (1960-2019), with expected ranges from eBird Status and Trends weekly abundance; vagrancy was a continuous index (mean distance to the 10 nearest abundance-weighted points). Geomagnetic disturbance (Ap index, 21-day rolling mean) had a strong autumn effect (posterior median 0.109, 95 percent CI 0.077 to 0.142) and a negligible spring effect (0.006); a two-SD rise in disturbance was associated with a 24 percent increase in the median vagrancy index and raised the share of spatiotemporally rare records from 2 to 5 percent; solar activity dampened the effect. The autumn model explained a median of 5.9 percent of variance (species range 0.3 to 30 percent); the authors state other factors likely explain 70 to more than 99 percent. Weather was not modelled. [verified: full text] [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9829733/)
- Tonelli maintains a "Rare Bird Forecast (BETA)" web tool that predicts vagrant appearances in spring and autumn migration from current geomagnetic conditions "and other stuff"; the page gives no lead time, no skill statement and no validation. [verified: page] [Source](https://bentonelli.github.io/rarebirdforecast/)

Synthesis sources
- Lees and Gilroy, "Vagrancy in Birds" (Helm 2021; Princeton 2022): a 62-page synthesis plus 259 pages of family accounts and over 700 references; mechanisms covered include compass errors, wind drift, overshooting, extreme weather, irruptions, dispersal and ship assistance; the review notes vagrancy "was once assumed to come from weather alone, but research now examines many factors". [verified: review] [Source](https://www.10000birds.com/vagrancy-in-birds-a-book-review.htm); [Source](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/85379)
- Dufour, Lees, Gilroy and Crochet (2023, Trends in Ecology and Evolution 39: 19-22) describe vagrancy events as "rare and unpredictable" while arguing they matter for eco-evolutionary processes. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2023.10.001)
- Coastal concentration in the Netherlands: a 1993 Groningen study (De Grauwe Gors) found that coastal areas where migrants are funnelled are the top locations for vagrants, and that most vagrants are birds that went off course during migration. [snippet] [Source](https://natuurtijdschriften.nl/pub/550011/GRGO1993021003013.pdf)

### Inferences
- The literature supports a "season x synoptic pattern x species pool" model of arrival risk: for each guild (Nearctic landbirds, Siberian passerines, southern overshoots) a characteristic pressure pattern and wind sector raises the odds, and the species pool is weighted by source population size and migratory direction (McLaren 2006; Thorup 2004; Zawadzki 2019). Nothing found quantifies day-level hit rates for this in Europe, so predictive skill at 1 to 7 days is an open empirical question for the Netherlands.
- Because observer coverage repeatedly appears as a top predictor of recorded vagrancy (Gilroy and Lees 2003; Thorup 2004; Bozó and Csörgő 2023), a heatmap trained on raw waarneming.nl records will largely learn where birders go unless effort is modelled explicitly (see Key question 2).
- The weak but real geomagnetic effect (Tonelli 2023) is a candidate low-cost covariate (Ap index forecasts are public) but should be expected to add little skill over weather and season.

### Gaps
- No European or Dutch study was found that reports out-of-sample skill (hit rate, AUC, Brier) for predicting rare-bird arrivals on a daily basis from weather. The Dutch Birding and Limosa archives were not searchable through the tools used; the "Recente meldingen" and CDNA annual reports were not consulted.
- Full texts of Elkins (1979), Lees and Gilroy (2009, chapter in Slack's "Rare Birds, Where and When") and Dufour et al. (2021, Current Biology) were not accessible; their weather rules and isotope results are cited only through secondary mentions.
- No study was found quantifying how often Nearctic landbirds reach the Netherlands (as opposed to Britain and Ireland) in relation to storm tracks.

## Key question 2: SDMs and occurrence models with citizen-science data, handling of effort and sampling bias, and what works with presence-only rare-bird reports

### Takeaway
The eBird school (AdaSTEM ensembles of trees, complete checklists, effort covariates, spatiotemporal subsampling, class balancing) is the best-documented recipe, and the largest measured gains come from using detection and non-detection data with effort covariates rather than presence-only data. With presence-only rarity reports and no effort data, the defensible options are target-group background (using all bird records as the effort proxy), joint multi-species effort estimation, or occupancy-style models on detection histories built from the complete record stream.

### Cited Findings

eBird Status and Trends and AdaSTEM
- Fink et al. (2020, Ecological Applications 30: e02056) present the framework used by eBird Status and Trends: year-round, range-wide estimates of occurrence and relative abundance, intra-annual range and habitat associations, and inter-annual trends, demonstrated on Wood Thrush. [verified: abstract (bioRxiv version)] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1002/eap.2056)
- AdaSTEM (Fink, Damoulas and Dave 2013, AAAI) is the adaptive spatiotemporal exploratory model that partitions space and time into overlapping stixels with local base models and "is able to efficiently discover multiscale structure when it is present, while retaining predictive performance when absent". [verified: abstract] [Source](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/8484)
- The 2020 Status and Trends data version changed the AdaSTEM temporal partition width from 30.5 to 28 days and the percent-above-threshold cutoff for presence from 0.1 to 0.143. [verified: changelog] [Source](https://ftp.eenet.ee/pub/cran/web/packages/ebirdst/vignettes/product-changelog.html)
- Status and Trends predictive performance metrics (PPMs) are computed on held-out checklists, subsampled spatiotemporally over 25 Monte Carlo iterations, in three groups: binary range metrics, occurrence metrics within range, and abundance metrics within range; the package also exposes Bernoulli and Poisson deviance; cutoffs (ensemble support, percent above threshold) are optimised per week. [verified: package documentation] [Source](https://rdrr.io/github/CornellLabofOrnithology/stemhelper/man/ebirdst_ppms.html)

Best-practice guidelines
- Johnston et al. (2021, Diversity and Distributions 27: 1265-1277; bioRxiv 574392) tested a ladder of analytical choices on Wood Thrush in June in BCR 27. Filters: complete stationary or travelling checklists, 2008 onward, duration at most 5 hours, distance at most 5 km, at most 10 observers. Subsampling: one detection and one non-detection per 5 km hexagon per week (dggridR), then an 80/20 split. Metrics: sensitivity, specificity, AUC, Kappa and MSE (Brier) for encounter rate; MSE and Spearman for abundance. Presence-only Maxent with random background had much lower AUC and very low specificity; the full workflow had the highest AUC and specificity; "the largest gap among models 2 to 6 came from adding effort covariates"; gains held at 10 to 100 percent of the data. Recommendations: use detections and non-detections, do not reduce checklists to presence-only, include effort and detectability covariates (time, date, duration, distance, protocol, observers, observer calibration index), combine filtering with covariate modelling. [verified: bioRxiv full text] [Source](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/574392v2.full-text); [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/ddi.13271)
- The online book "Best Practices for Using eBird Data" (v2.0, Strimas-Mackey, Johnston et al.) operationalises these steps in R (auk, ranger, mgcv, scam, PresenceAbsence) with encounter-rate and relative-abundance chapters. [verified: intro page] [Source](https://ebird.github.io/ebird-best-practices/)

Rare species and class imbalance
- Robinson, Ruiz-Gutierrez and Fink (2018, Diversity and Distributions) modelled Tricolored Blackbird in California with eBird: prevalence 1.7 percent in winter (1,900 detections on 108,880 checklists) and 1.3 percent in the breeding season (3,973 on 302,655). They compared random forest, weighted RF, balanced RF and SMOTE with and without spatial under-sampling of non-detections (one random non-detection per grid cell, keeping all detections); after spatial under-sampling the training data had 42 percent (breeding) and 35 percent (winter) detections. Metrics: OOB error, AUC, Brier score, sensitivity, specificity, Kappa, on 50 random splits with spatially balanced test sets. AUC was higher with spatial under-sampling but above 0.85 for all models; Brier score consistently improved with spatial under-sampling; sensitivity rose without loss of specificity; OOB error is "a poor measure of accuracy when using class-imbalanced data". [verified: full text] [Source](https://tricolor.ice.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk3096/files/inline-files/Robinson_et_al-2017-Diversity_and_Distributions.pdf)
- Steen, Tingley, Paton and Elphick (2021, Methods in Ecology and Evolution 12) compared spatial thinning, class balancing and majority-only thinning for 102 species with eBird data from the north-eastern USA: for most rare species, class balancing improved discrimination with machine-learning methods but generally hindered calibration; "there is no single best dataset", and baseline prevalence, sample size, modelling approach and whether discrimination or calibration is needed should drive the choice. [verified: blog summary and repository page] [Source](https://methodsblog.com/2022/04/14/spatial-thinning-and-class-balancing-key-choices-lead-to-variation-in-the-performance-of-species-distribution-models-with-citizen-science-data/); [Source](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/nrs_facpubs/679)

Spatial bias and effort when effort is unknown
- Phillips et al. (2009, Ecological Applications) proposed target-group background: because an entire group of species observed by similar methods shares the same spatial bias, all occurrences within the target group can serve as biased background. Across 226 species, target-group background improved average performance for all methods, "with the choice of background data having as large an effect on predictive performance as the choice of modelling method", and the gain was largest under strong bias. Applies to GLM, GAM, boosted regression trees and Maxent. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1890/07-2153.1)
- Fithian et al. (2015, Methods in Ecology and Evolution 6) pool presence-only and presence-absence data for many species in a joint likelihood that estimates a shared sampling bias; for 36 eucalypts the presence-only records were strongly biased toward the coast and Sydney; "if we have only presence-only data ... for a given species, but both types of data for several other species that suffer from the same spatial sampling bias, then our method can obtain an unbiased estimate of the first species' geographic range". [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12242)
- Botella et al. (2021, Methods in Ecology and Evolution 12: 933-945) estimate spatial sampling effort over a mesh jointly with environmental densities of multiple species using a marked Poisson process on opportunistic presence-only data; "a few frequently observed species greatly contribute to correctly estimate sampling effort, thereby improving density estimation of all other species". [verified: abstract] [Source](https://hal.umontpellier.fr/hal-03150701/document)
- Isaac et al. (2020, Trends in Ecology and Evolution) frame data integration with point process models as the general way to combine structured and opportunistic data. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2019.08.006)
- Johnston et al. (2020, Ecological Modelling 422) modelled site-selection bias in BirdTrack (Great Britain, 2007-2011) and compared occupancy models for 138 species with Atlas data: spatially biased data gave accurate and precise occupancy for most of Britain, but less accurate and positively biased estimates in the Scottish Highlands (environmentally distinct, few observations); weighting for bias improved accuracy there but not precision. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolmodel.2019.108927)
- Van Strien, Van Swaay and Termaat (2013, Journal of Applied Ecology 50: 1450-1458), using Dutch opportunistic butterfly and dragonfly data, showed that occupancy models on detection/non-detection data correct for observation, reporting and detection bias; "distribution trends in opportunistic and monitoring data were well-matched" and "strong trends observed in monitoring data were rarely missed in opportunistic data". [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2664.12158)
- A simulation study with real-world sampling patterns (PeerJ 2020) found spatial bias reduced SDM prediction performance "but only when the bias was relatively strong"; sample size and modelling method mattered more. [snippet] [Source](https://peerj.com/articles/10411)
- Spatial bias in citizen-science records is driven mainly by accessibility (path density was the strongest predictor for seven of eight taxa including birds) and presence of water bodies. [snippet; source attribution not verified] [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5643322)

Real-time citizen-science forecasting
- Ovaskainen et al. (2026, Nature Ecology and Evolution, "A digital twin for real-time biodiversity forecasting with citizen science data") built a continuously updating model in Finland from smartphone audio classified by machine learning (15 million detections in 2 years), counteracting "spatiotemporal sampling biases by interval recordings and permanent point count networks"; independent test data show the digital-twin-informed models "are more accurate at predicting bird spatiotemporal distributions". Predictions update daily on CSC supercomputers; the press release gives no AUC or other numbers. [verified: abstract; press release] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-025-02966-3); [Source](https://www.jyu.fi/en/news/the-worlds-most-accurate-model-for-predicting-bird-occurrence-is-based-on-the-pioneering-work-of)

### Inferences
- waarneming.nl and observation.org records are presence-only at the record level, but the full stream of common-species records per observer per day can be converted into pseudo-checklists (observer-day-location units) that give detection/non-detection data for rarities, which is exactly the structure the eBird results favour over presence-only. This is an inference from Johnston et al. (2021) and van Strien et al. (2013), not something those papers tested on observation.org data.
- If that conversion is not feasible, the all-bird-records density per cell per week is a target-group background in the sense of Phillips et al. (2009) and is the cheapest effort proxy; Botella et al. (2021) and Fithian et al. (2015) show that common species can be used to estimate the effort surface jointly with the rare species.
- Robinson et al. (2018) and Steen et al. (2021) jointly imply: balance classes if you want ranking (top-k hotspots), but recalibrate afterwards if you want probabilities people can read as such.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed paper was found that builds SDMs specifically from observation.org or waarneming.nl bird data with an explicit effort correction; the Dutch examples found (van Strien 2013; later plant monitoring work) are for insects and plants.
- The exact list of eBird Status and Trends PPMs (which binary and abundance metrics) and the minimum data thresholds for a species to be modelled were not verified (the FAQ page could not be fetched).

## Key question 3: Short-term (1 to 7 day) spatio-temporal forecasting, persistence of individual rarities, site fidelity and "where will it go next"

### Takeaway
Day-ahead forecasting of bird movement from numerical weather prediction is proven for bulk migration (62 to 76 percent of variance explained at 1 to 7 days in the USA; 71 percent in the Dutch North Sea) and for one rare species with a strong weather signal (Ross's Gull, 1 to 5 days ahead). For individual staked-out rarities the main published finding is that apparent stay lengths are inflated by observer effort; no multi-species stay-length distribution or "next site" model was found.

### Cited Findings

Weather-driven short-term forecasts
- Van Doren and Horton (2018, Science 361) trained models on 23 years of spring radar data from 143 NEXRAD stations with atmospheric predictors; models explained up to 81 percent of variation in migration intensity at 0 to 3,000 m, and "performance remained high in forecasting events 1 to 7 days in advance (62 to 76 percent of variation was explained)"; the preprint quoted 24 to 72 h. [verified: open copy] [Source](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10092591); [Source](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat7526); [Source](https://birdcast.org/creating-a-continental-bird-migration-forecast/)
- Bradarić, Kranstauber, Bouten and Shamoun-Baranes (2024, Journal of Applied Ecology) forecast hourly low-altitude nocturnal migration over the southern North Sea from a Robin radar at Luchterduinen (23 km off the Dutch coast; spring 2019-2023, autumn 2019-2022) with ERA5 predictors (wind assistance by season and departure region, precipitation, temperature, mean sea-level pressure, nightly differences, diurnal and seasonal phenology proxies). Models explained 71 percent of variance and classified hours above or below the intense-migration threshold correctly in more than 80 percent of hours, but "the percentage of correctly predicted intense migration hours was low" (the top 5 percent), attributed to the short dataset and rarity of such hours. Curtailing turbines for about 18 hours in spring and 26 in autumn would protect 50 percent of migrants at 0.12 percent of annual energy. [verified: Dryad abstract and README] [Source](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.8gtht76x0); [Source](https://bes.brightminded.com/applied-ecology-resources/document/20240382115)
- The operational Dutch version of that model (University of Amsterdam, used for offshore wind curtailment) is a machine-learning model with separate spring and autumn modules that must predict migration peaks 48 hours in advance; a 2023 validation report exists. [snippet] [Source](https://www.noordzeeloket.nl/publish/pages/236249/validation-of-the-outcomes-of-the-bird-migration-prediction-model-for-2023.pdf)
- Senzaki et al. (2024, Ecology and Evolution 14: e11388) built two forecast systems for daily sighting probability of Ross's Gull at the Shiretoko Peninsula, Japan, from 16 winters of counts: Method A classified sea-level-pressure patterns over the Sea of Okhotsk into 16 self-organizing-map nodes (sighting probability 0 to 85.7 percent by node); Method B was a GLM on a 24 h backward wind trajectory latitude (strong northerlies), ordinal date and observation duration (0 to 95.5 percent at 60 min effort). Both were driven by the JMA operational global NWP model to give 1 to 5 day forecasts, "determined based on the data availability of the operational numerical weather prediction model". Validation was qualitative because "the Brier score ... requires many results"; in a 2-year follow-up gulls were seen on 6 of 14 forecast-guided days, with sighted days having higher probabilities across the 5-day window and unsighted days below 10 percent in at least one system. First detection on presence days took 14.48 plus or minus 28.33 min, so effort over 45 min was treated as sufficient. [verified: full text] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.11388); [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11199128/)
- A 2025 Scientific Reports study trained random forests on climate data to predict eBird occurrence rates of 41 migrating passerines and found lagged climate features alone gave more accurate October predictions. [snippet] [Source](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-06961-3)
- Random forests on Trektellen and eBird seawatch counts explained over 52 percent of variance in Balearic Shearwater abundance during migration. [snippet] [Source](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7428152/)

Population-flow forecasts from eBird
- BirdFlow (Fuentes, Van Doren, Fink and Sheldon 2023, Methods in Ecology and Evolution 14) is a probabilistic framework that infers weekly population flows from eBird Status and Trends relative abundance; applied to 11 North American species and tuned and evaluated with GPS and satellite tracks; "BirdFlow models can accurately infer individual seasonal movement behavior directly from eBird relative abundance estimates", tracking data improve it, and outputs include routes, timing, connectivity and forecasts (examples at 3, 6 and 12 week horizons). [verified: abstract; figure captions via search] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.14052); [Source](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.04.12.488057v2)

Persistence and stay length of individual rarities
- Ahmed (2025, British Birds 118(5), "Observer effort and its influence on apparent stay lengths of vagrant North American landbirds") compared five species between Britain and Iceland and between the Isles of Scilly and the Outer Hebrides and found "longer apparent stay lengths in more heavily birded regions compared to more sparsely watched ones at both scales", attributing this to resighting and detection rates and warning that low coverage "likely results in underestimation of true stay durations". A reply letter notes most Nearctic vagrants are carried by mid-latitude weather systems rather than making first landfall in Iceland. [snippet; abstract only, full text paywalled] [Source](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/observer-effort-and-its-influence-apparent-stay-lengths-vagrant-north-american); [Source](https://d8.britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/vagrant-north-american-landbirds)
- Tryjanowski et al. (2024, People and Nature 6: 2390-2398) modelled birder crowd size at 103 observations of 71 rare species in Poland (1996-2022) against the number of days the bird stayed, distance to the nearest city and species rarity; crowds were larger for rarer species and closer to cities, and the share of observers who saw the bird rose at weekends. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doaj.org/article/8f2a9284447549e3a0bd66490aba4d48)
- A University of Reading economic analysis of twitching notes that rarities that stay for weeks lose some desirability and that species can be "effectively untwitchable" when individuals never stay beyond a single day (Alpine Accentor example). [snippet] [Source](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/103450/1/Economic%20Analysis%20Vagrant%20Birds%20May%202020%20Final.pdf)

### Inferences
- A practical 1 to 7 day rarity heatmap decomposes into (a) an arrival hazard driven by season and forecast weather (the Senzaki and Bradarić designs transfer directly: cluster forecast pressure fields or use wind-trajectory covariates from ECMWF or KNMI HARMONIE), (b) a persistence term for birds already found, and (c) an effort or detection term (weekend effect and distance to cities are documented in Tryjanowski 2024). This decomposition is my synthesis; no paper was found that builds all three for rarities.
- Stay-length hazards should be estimated with effort in mind (Ahmed 2025): raw "last seen" dates from waarneming.nl under-estimate stays at poorly watched sites, so a survival model with observer density as a covariate is more honest than a single median.
- The fact that bulk migration intensity is predictable at 1 to 7 days (Van Doren and Horton 2018) and that Bradarić et al. (2024) still failed on the rarest 5 percent of hours is a warning: rarity arrivals are the tail of the distribution, and tail events are where weather-driven models lose skill fastest.

### Gaps
- No published distribution of stay lengths for rarities (median days, by guild or season) was retrievable; Ahmed (2025) contains such data but the full text is paywalled.
- No study was found on movements of individual vagrants between nearby sites or on "next site" prediction; this appears to be unstudied in the peer-reviewed literature.
- The Noordzeeloket validation report (2023) was not opened, so the 48 h skill numbers of the operational Dutch migration model are not reported here.

## Key question 4: Irruption and influx forecasting, and lead times

### Takeaway
Irruptions of boreal seed and fruit eaters are predictable seasons ahead from food crops and large-scale climate dipoles in North America, where an operational Winter Finch Forecast has run since 1999 on cone and fruit surveys; in Europe, Bohemian Waxwing irruption numbers are synchronous across northern Europe and relate to Finnish rowan crops in a more complex way than the "no food, so they leave" rule suggests. No validated skill figures for any irruption forecast were found.

### Cited Findings
- Strong, Zuckerberg, Betancourt and Koenig (2015, PNAS 112) analysed more than 2 million Pine Siskin observations from Project FeederWatch and found two irruption modes (North-South and West-East; the latter 13 percent of variance) correlated with climate variability; the predominant drivers are warm-season continental-scale dipoles of precipitation and temperature anomalies that juxtapose favourable and unfavourable conditions for seed production, motivating a push-pull paradigm. [verified: abstract and full text excerpts] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418414112) Press materials claimed irruptions might be predicted "up to two years in advance" in principle; no validated forecast record was found. [press release] [Source](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/news/?p=19948)
- A follow-up NSF project describes irruptions as triggered by seed crop failures when the North Atlantic Oscillation and North Pacific Oscillation are simultaneously in positive phases. [snippet] [Source](https://par.nsf.gov/search/award_ids:2525033)
- The Winter Finch Forecast (started by Ron Pittaway, now compiled by Tyler Hoar for the Finch Research Network) has since 1999 predicted which finches move south from cone, seed and fruit crops surveyed in Canada the previous growing season; it covers Purple Finch, Pine Siskin, Evening and Pine Grosbeak, Red and White-winged Crossbill and redpolls, is designed for southern Ontario and the north-eastern USA, and publishes no accuracy statistics. [snippet] [Source](https://finchnetwork.org/qa-with-tyler-hoar-compiler-of-the-winter-finch-forecast); [Source](https://finchnetwork.org/winter-finch-forecast-update); [Source](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/news/redpolls-grosbeaks-finches-forecast-winter-2026/)
- Dale (2023, Journal of Ornithology) used up to 41 years of Bohemian Waxwing numbers (breeding in Finland and Sweden; winter in Norway, Sweden, Denmark, UK and Poland from online portals, corrected by total record numbers; Norway r = 0.97 between birds and records) and rowanberry indices from Norway and Finland (masting every 2 to 3 years; rowan masting synchronous up to 1,000 km). Winter numbers fluctuated in synchrony over northern Europe; low Finnish rowan abundance led to early arrival in the south (partial r = -0.57, n = 29 years, p < 0.001) and early arrival meant larger numbers in Denmark (partial r = 0.45) and Poland (partial r = 0.70) but not in Norway, Sweden or the UK; controlling for arrival timing, numbers were larger when Finland had more rowanberries; "most irruptions occurred in years with high rowanberry abundance in autumn in northern areas. This is opposite to expectations from the current view that irruptions are triggered by lack of food"; no support for a southern "pull" by berries; mixed evidence for a population-density push. [verified: full text] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10336-023-02083-7); [Source](https://www.dof.dk/images/projekter/punkttaelling/dokumenter/Dale-2023_silkehale.pdf)
- The 1965/66 waxwing invasion in Britain and Europe followed a mild autumn with an abundant rowan crop in Scandinavia and had been flagged in advance by Kai Curry-Lindahl from breeding-season observations. [snippet] [Source](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/waxwings-britain-and-europe-during-196566)
- Sovon (Netherlands): a combination of many birds after good breeding success and few berries signals large waxwing movements; waxwings are extremely rare outside October to April and usually most numerous in November and December, in some invasions only in February; large Parrot Crossbill invasions occurred in 1982/83 and 1990/91 with a smaller one in 2013/14; Sovon's Ruud Foppen names Scandinavian food supply, not temperature, as the main driver of invasions. [snippet] [Source](https://stats.sovon.nl/stats/soort/10480); [Source](https://stats.sovon.nl/stats/soort/16680); [Source](https://www.bnnvara.nl/vroegevogels/artikelen/waar-zijn-de-wintergasten)

### Inferences
- For a Dutch heatmap, irruptive species (waxwing, crossbills, nutcracker, Pallas's Leaf Warbler as a quasi-irruptive drift migrant) are best handled as a seasonal prior updated by early-season counts from Fennoscandia and northern Germany (Dale 2023 shows synchrony across the region, and early arrival in the south is itself an informative signal), rather than by day-level weather.
- Lead time for irruptions is therefore weeks to months (crop and breeding information), which is a different product from a 1 to 7 day rarity forecast; the two should not share one model.

### Gaps
- No European equivalent of the Winter Finch Forecast with a published method was found, and no study quantifies forecast skill for waxwing, crossbill or nutcracker irruptions reaching the Netherlands.
- Pallas's Leaf Warbler and nutcracker influx analyses with weather in Limosa or Dutch Birding were not located with the tools available.

## Key question 5: Evaluation metrics, reported skill levels and baselines

### Takeaway
Detection/non-detection work uses AUC, Kappa, sensitivity, specificity and Brier score (eBird) with spatiotemporally subsampled hold-outs; presence-only work should use the continuous Boyce index because AUC is prevalence-dependent and misleading there. Reported skill: AUC above 0.85 for a rare blackbird with eBird; 62 to 76 percent variance explained for 1 to 7 day migration forecasts; qualitative validation only for the one rare-species forecast; no paper found reports skill of a rarity model against persistence or climatology baselines.

### Cited Findings
- Hirzel et al. (2006, Ecological Modelling) tested Kappa, MaxKappa, AUC and adjusted D2 against presence-only measures (AVI, CVI, Boyce index) on 114 plant species and introduced the continuous Boyce index; presence-only evaluators correlated with presence/absence ones (rho above 0.7), the Boyce indices were closer to AUC than to MaxKappa and "fairly insensitive to species prevalence"; the predicted-to-expected ratio curve also helps reclassify maps into suitability classes. [verified: PDF summary] [Source](https://www.unil.ch/biomapper/Download/Hirzel-EcoMod-2006.pdf)
- Lobo, Jiménez-Valverde and Real (2008, Global Ecology and Biogeography 17, "AUC: a misleading measure of the performance of predictive distribution models") is the standard critique. [title and year verified via Crossref; abstract not retrieved] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1466-8238.2007.00358.x)
- van Proosdij et al. (2016, Ecography 39) state that "when applied to presence-only data [AUC] is prevalence-dependent and hence not an accurate performance index" and recommend testing AUC for significant deviation from random instead. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.01509)
- eBird practice: Robinson et al. (2018) report Kappa, Brier score and AUC on 50 spatially balanced test splits, with thresholds chosen to maximise Kappa per split; Johnston et al. (2021) report sensitivity, specificity, AUC, Kappa and MSE (Brier) for encounter rate and MSE and Spearman for abundance; Status and Trends uses 25 Monte Carlo spatiotemporal subsamples of held-out checklists. [verified: full texts and documentation] [Source](https://tricolor.ice.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk3096/files/inline-files/Robinson_et_al-2017-Diversity_and_Distributions.pdf); [Source](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/574392v2.full-text); [Source](https://rdrr.io/github/CornellLabofOrnithology/stemhelper/man/ebirdst_ppms.html)
- Steen et al. (2021) make the discrimination versus calibration distinction explicit: class balancing improves discrimination but hinders calibration for rare species. [verified: summary] [Source](https://methodsblog.com/2022/04/14/spatial-thinning-and-class-balancing-key-choices-lead-to-variation-in-the-performance-of-species-distribution-models-with-citizen-science-data/)
- Senzaki et al. (2024) note that the Brier score is the common metric for probabilistic forecasts but needs many forecast instances; with few rare-event days they fell back on case-by-case comparison of forecast probability trajectories over 1 to 5 day lead times. [verified: full text] [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11199128/)
- Skill levels reported: AUC above 0.85 for all models of a 1.3 to 1.7 percent prevalence species (Robinson et al. 2018); 62 to 76 percent of variance in migration intensity at 1 to 7 day lead (Van Doren and Horton 2018); 71 percent variance and more than 80 percent correct threshold classification but poor recall on the top 5 percent of hours (Bradarić et al. 2024); 5.9 percent median variance explained for geomagnetic vagrancy effect (Tonelli et al. 2023). [verified as cited above]

### Inferences
- For a presence-only rarity heatmap, the Boyce index (ranked suitability versus observed rarity records) and a top-k hit rate (share of new rarities falling in the top k percent of cells on the day they were found) are the metrics that match both the data type and the use case; Brier or log-loss and a Brier skill score against a climatology baseline are appropriate if the pseudo-checklist route from Key question 2 is taken.
- Two baselines follow from the literature even though no rarity paper reports them: climatology (per species, week of year, cell, from historical records normalised by effort) and persistence (yesterday's known birds stay). A weather-driven model should be shown to beat both before it is called a forecast.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that reports top-k hit rate, log-loss or calibration for any rare-bird or vagrant forecasting model, nor a skill comparison against persistence or climatology for such a model.

## Key question 6: Data requirements for rare-species SDMs and techniques for data-poor species

### Takeaway
Absolute minima of roughly 14 to 25 records are reported for presence-only SDMs in a realistic landscape, with no algorithm reliable below about 30 records; ensembles of small models, class rebalancing with spatial filtering, joint or multi-species models that borrow strength, and more recently few-shot neural range models are the tools for species below those counts.

### Cited Findings
- Wisz et al. (2008, Diversity and Distributions 14) evaluated 12 algorithms for 46 species at 100, 30 and 10 records: accuracy fell and variability rose with decreasing sample size; GBM, MARS-INT and BRUTO did best at large n but not at the smallest; Maxent was among the best across all sizes; "no algorithm predicted consistently well with small sample size (n < 30)", so such predictions should be restricted to exploratory use. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1472-4642.2008.00482.x)
- van Proosdij et al. (2016, Ecography 39) used simulated species to find minimum record counts by prevalence class: as low as 3 (narrow-ranged) and 13 (widespread) in an idealised virtual area (the 3 is a statistical artefact below prevalence 0.1), and 14 (narrow-ranged) to 25 (widespread) in a real African study area; performance rises with sample size at constant prevalence and falls with prevalence at constant sample size. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.01509)
- Breiner et al. (2015, Methods in Ecology and Evolution 6): ensembles of small (bivariate) models weighted by AUC, tested on 107 rare Swiss plants with GLM, GBM and Maxent, "performed significantly better than standard SDMs. The rarer the species, the more pronounced the effects were", also under a transferability assessment; ESMs avoid overfitting without discarding predictors. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12403)
- Erickson and Smith (2022, bioRxiv) compared joint SDMs, ESMs and single-species models at N = 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64 occurrences of virtual species embedded in a real community: ESMs gave the best discrimination for most species and better-than-random accuracy even at N = 2; JSDMs and single-species models recovered response curves better but flagged unimportant variables at low N; species with narrow, peripheral niches were easier regardless of method; "the best algorithm depends on number of occurrences and whether the niche or distribution is the focus". Traditional minima quoted in the literature range from about 5 to more than 200. [verified: preprint abstract and summary] [Source](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.21.497071v1)
- Robinson et al. (2018) showed that with roughly 1,900 to 3,973 detections in 109k to 303k eBird checklists, spatial under-sampling of non-detections plus random forest gives AUC above 0.85; this is the eBird-style route for rare but not extremely rare species. [verified: full text] [Source](https://tricolor.ice.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk3096/files/inline-files/Robinson_et_al-2017-Diversity_and_Distributions.pdf)
- Borrowing strength across species: Fithian et al. (2015) pool many species to estimate shared bias and can estimate a species' range from presence-only data alone when other species have both data types; Botella et al. (2021) use frequently observed species to estimate effort for all species. [verified: abstracts] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12242); [Source](https://hal.umontpellier.fr/hal-03150701/document)
- Neural multi-species range models: Cole et al. (2023, ICML) train spatial implicit neural representations on crowdsourced data for about 47,000 species at once, improving as species and data per species grow, and show noisy biased crowdsourced data can approximate expert range maps for many species; the follow-up FS-SINR (Lange et al. 2025, ICML) targets the few-shot regime and can estimate a previously unseen species' range in a single forward pass. Exact few-shot numbers were not verified. [snippet] [Source](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/cole23a.html); [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14977v2)
- Trait-based pooling of rarities is implicit in McLaren et al. (2006), who used classification and regression trees on body mass, wing length, migratory span, breeding latitude and day/night migration to cluster potential transatlantic vagrants into homogeneous groups. [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1474-919X.2006.00574.x)

### Inferences
- Most CDNA-level rarities in the Netherlands have far fewer than 25 records in any cell-week stratum, so per-species SDMs are out of reach; the literature points to modelling at the level of guilds defined by origin and migration direction (Nearctic, Siberian, southern overshoot, irruptive), with species-level priors from traits and source population size (McLaren 2006; Thorup 2004; Zawadzki 2019), and ESM-style simple models rather than deep feature sets.
- Transfer from neighbouring regions is plausible because the same weather systems and coasts act on Britain, Belgium, Germany and Denmark, but no paper tested cross-border transfer of a rarity model; it should be treated as a hypothesis to evaluate.

### Gaps
- No study quantifies minimum record counts specifically for vagrant birds or for space-time (weekly) models; the thresholds above are for static presence-only SDMs of plants and other taxa.
- Hierarchical or joint models applied to vagrant birds specifically were not found.

## Key question 7: Papers specifically about predicting rarity occurrence for birders

### Takeaway
Only one peer-reviewed operational rarity forecast exists (Ross's Gull, Japan, 1 to 5 days ahead from NWP), plus a beta geomagnetic "Rare Bird Forecast" web tool without published validation; the European birder-facing literature is heuristic (British Birds and BirdGuides weather-pattern articles) and strongly caveated, and no paper on rarity hotspots or vagrant forecasting for the Netherlands was found.

### Cited Findings
- Senzaki et al. (2024) explicitly frame their work as a "rare bird forecast" for wildlife observers: "predicting the sighting probabilities of these animals is a challenge for many observers, and it may only be possible by limited experts", and they cite Gilroy and Lees (2003) and Lees and Gilroy (2009) for rarity likelihood rising with "wind drift, squalls, and cyclone passages, and the developed low-pressure systems". [verified: full text] [Source](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.11388)
- Tonelli's "Rare Bird Forecast (BETA)" predicts vagrant appearances during migration from current geomagnetic conditions, citing Tonelli et al. (2023) and a PNAS radar paper (doi 10.1073/pnas.2306317120) for method; no skill or lead time is stated. [verified: page] [Source](https://bentonelli.github.io/rarebirdforecast/)
- BirdGuides "Predicting Nearctic passerines in autumn" presents the Elkins and Lees and Gilroy weather theory as a practical guide with the caveat of lower reliability than for Palearctic arrivals. [snippet] [Source](https://www.birdguides.com/articles/migration/predicting-nearctic-passerines-in-autumn/)
- Hotspot geography in birding sources: the Isles of Scilly are called "still arguably the UK's top rarity hotspot" (Bird Watching magazine), and North Ronaldsay "averages at least one mega vagrant every year" while being overshadowed by Fair Isle; these are observer-dense island observatories. [snippet] [Source](https://www.birdguides.com/articles/rare-birds/britain-ireland/a-magical-autumn-on-north-ronaldsay/)
- A Nova Scotia island study argues the incidence of vagrants there is higher than elsewhere in eastern North America, and a California study found vagrancy strongly correlated with larger North American population size. [snippet] [Source](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/auk/v098n02/p0243-p0257.pdf)
- Tryjanowski et al. (2024) is the only paper found that treats rarity records from the birder side (crowd size versus stay length, distance to city, rarity, weekend). [verified: abstract] [Source](https://doaj.org/article/8f2a9284447549e3a0bd66490aba4d48)

### Inferences
- The field is open: a Dutch rarity heatmap with honest effort correction, a climatology baseline and published hit rates would be a first in the peer-reviewed sense, and the Senzaki design (NWP-driven, two independent methods, probabilities shown per lead day) is the closest template.
- Because the best-known "hotspots" are also the best-watched places, any hotspot map derived from records needs an effort denominator before it can claim that a site attracts rarities rather than birders (Gilroy and Lees 2003; Ahmed 2025).

### Gaps
- No academic or grey-literature paper on "rarity hotspots" or "vagrancy hotspots" for the Netherlands (Texel, Vlieland, Maasvlakte, Zuid-Limburg) was found; CDNA annual reports and Dutch Birding statistics were not accessible through the tools used and should be consulted directly.
- The PNAS 2023 radar paper cited on the Rare Bird Forecast page was not retrieved.
