# Species-level ecology and trait data for European birds (movement, habitat, phenology, vagrant behaviour)

Research date: 24 October 2026. Scope: Europe, target region the Netherlands, species list of waarneming.nl (roughly 1000 bird species with Dutch names). Audience: a hobby developer building a test "rare bird occurrence heatmap" that spreads recent sightings over the map by daily travel capacity, habitat and season.

Verification legend used below: "verified" means the page or file was fetched and read during this research; "search snippet" means the claim comes from a search-result summary and the underlying page was not opened; "from memory" means prior knowledge that could not be checked against a live page. Several key sites blocked automated access (Figshare file downloads, britishbirds.co.uk, trektellen.nl, api.iucnredlist.org, avibase, Wiley); EURING's website was in maintenance mode and migrationatlas.org refused connections at the time of research. These are flagged where relevant.

## Key question 1: Trait databases (fields, licence, format, taxonomy) and joining to waarneming.nl Dutch names

### Takeaway
Three open datasets cover nearly every European species at the species level: AVONET (CC BY 4.0, morphology plus coarse habitat, migration and trophic categories, three taxonomies), EltonTraits (no copyright restrictions, diet and foraging-stratum scores, BirdLife v3 taxonomy) and Storchová and Hořák 2018 (CC0, 499 European breeding species, 85 variables from BWP). The cleanest join key to waarneming.nl is the scientific name, because the site's species API returns `scientific_name`, localised `name` (Dutch with `Accept-Language: nl`), `rarity` and `status`, and states that its taxonomy follows AviList; AviList v2026 (CC BY 4.0, XLSX) carries cross-references to BirdLife v10 and eBird/Clements v2026 names, which bridges to AVONET's three taxonomies and to BirdLife/IUCN data.

AVONET (Tobias et al. 2022)
- The Figshare record "AVONET: morphological, ecological and geographical data for all birds (Tobias et al 2022 Ecology Letters)" is licensed CC BY 4.0, DOI 10.6084/m9.figshare.16586228.v7, version published 2022-12-05; files are "AVONET Supplementary dataset 1.xlsx" (21.5 MB, full dataset with raw measurements and species averages per taxonomy), "Supplementary dataset 2.xlsx" (duplicate measurements), "ELEData.zip" and "ELECode.zip" (verified via the Figshare v2 API, https://api.figshare.com/v2/articles/16586228): [Figshare record](https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/AVONET_morphological_ecological_and_geographical_data_for_all_birds_Tobias_et_al_2021_Ecology_Letters_/16586228)
- The record description states: "six ecological variables, eleven continuous morphological traits, and information on range size and location. Raw morphological measurements are available from 90020 individuals of 11009 extant bird species sampled from 181 countries. These data are also summarised as species averages in three taxonomic formats, allowing integration with a global phylogeny, geographical range maps, IUCN Red List data, and the eBird citizen science database" (verified via API): [Figshare record](https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/AVONET_morphological_ecological_and_geographical_data_for_all_birds_Tobias_et_al_2021_Ecology_Letters_/16586228)

- The hand-wing index is computed as 100 × Kipp's distance / wing length and is used as a proxy for flight efficiency and dispersal ability (search snippet from an R package manual that uses AVONET): [PNC package manual](https://cran.r-universe.dev/PNC/doc/manual.html)
- Note on the migration field: a secondary source states that AVONET's migration category derives from BirdLife, with only full migrants coded as migratory (search snippet; verify against the AVONET metadata sheet): [search result](https://www.countryside-jobs.com/article/2022-02-24-body-measurements-for-all-11000-bird-species-released-in-open-access-database)

Treat this list as unverified until the "Metadata" sheet of Supplementary dataset 1 has been read.

- Dryad dataset doi 10.5061/dryad.n6k3n, version 2018-11-16; licence CC0 1.0 (https://spdx.org/licenses/CC0-1.0.html), verified through the Dryad v2 API; authors Lenka Storchová and David Hořák (Charles University); companion paper in Global Ecology and Biogeography, doi 10.1111/geb.12709: [Dryad API record](https://datadryad.org/api/v2/datasets/doi%3A10.5061%2Fdryad.n6k3n)

### Inferences
- A practical join pipeline: waarneming.nl species id -> `scientific_name` (AviList taxonomy) -> AviList v2026 Extended sheet (which carries BirdLife v10 and eBird/Clements v2026 names and Cornell species codes) -> AVONET1_BirdLife and AVONET2_eBird species names, EltonTraits `Scientific` (BirdLife v3, so expect a few hundred renamed or split taxa needing manual synonyms), and Storchová and Hořák names (BWP-era names, expect splits such as Saxicola, Phylloscopus and Larus to need mapping).
- Because waarneming.nl's documented API is limited to approved applications, a hobby project should either request access through the contact form or rely on a one-off manual mapping; the unauthenticated per-id responses observed here may not be stable or permitted for bulk use.
- For a hobby, non-commercial heatmap, AVONET, EltonTraits, Storchová and Hořák, AviList and Movebank Data Repository material can be stored and redistributed; BirdLife DataZone and IUCN Red List content can be used only under their request-based, non-commercial, no-redistribution terms, and scraping DataZone is explicitly forbidden.

## Key question 2: Daily movement and migration distances per species or group

### Takeaway
The only per-species table of migration speed (km/day) that covers many European species in one place is Payevsky's 2013 review of ring-recovery data for 115 species (verified from the PDF): most species average 20 to 100 km/day, raptors about 132, shorebirds about 117 and passerines about 56 km/day, with maxima typically 40 to 360 km/day. Tracking-based sources (Alerstam airspeeds, Schmaljohann's songbird tracks, crane, goose and raptor GPS studies) refine groups but no open, complete per-species km/day table exists; the Eurasian African Bird Migration Atlas is the most promising source but was unreachable.

### Cited Findings

Payevsky 2013 (ring-recovery review, verified from the full PDF)
- Citation: V. A. Payevsky, "Speed of Bird Migratory Movements as an Adaptive Behavior", Biology Bulletin Reviews 2013, Vol. 3, No. 3, pp. 219-231, doi 10.1134/S2079086413030079 (original Russian in Zhurnal Obshchei Biologii 2012, 73(5):360-376): [PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)
- "The migration speed of 115 bird species from 35 families of 14 orders has been analyzed on the basis of ring recovery data ... the average speed varies between 10 and 880 km/day and the maximum speed varies between 30 and 1392 km/day, but the respective values in the majority of species fall within the ranges of 20-100 and 40-360 km/day ... the overall average ratio [max:average] being 4 : 1": [PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)
- Group means: "132.2 ± 20.9 km/day in birds of prey, 116.5 ± 15.5 km/day in shorebirds, and 56.4 ± 3.0 km/day in passerines" (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.004): [PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)
- Family means for passerines: Turdidae (9 species) 58 ± 4, Fringillidae (7) 43 ± 4, Paridae (4) 24 ± 2, Sylviidae (9) 66 ± 3, Motacillidae (4) 63 ± 4 km/day: [PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)
- Early-departing nocturnal long-distance passerines (19 species: Sylviidae, Muscicapidae, Laniidae, Turdidae) 66.9 ± 3.7 vs early-departing diurnal migrants (14 species: Sturnidae, Fringillidae, Paridae) 35.1 ± 3.4 km/day; irruptive or irregular migrants (Nutcracker, Coal Tit, Long-tailed Tit, Waxwing, Bullfinch) 34.2 ± 4.2 vs regular diurnal migrants (Starling, Chaffinch, Brambling) 51.3 ± 2.2 km/day; Newton (2008) medians cited: 75 km/day for long-distance tropical winterers, 53 for short-distance temperate winterers, 27 for invasive species and partial migrants: [PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)
- Speed increases with route length: about 80 km/day for 1000 km routes to about 140 km/day for 5000-6000 km; Willow Warbler averaged 41 km/day over 400-1000 km, 54-59 over 1000-3000 km and 85 km/day beyond 3000 km (Hedenström and Pettersson 1987); spring is faster than autumn in several species; adults faster than juveniles in many species: [PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)
- Selected per-species values from the table (average km/day, with maxima), all verified: White Stork 54 (max 42-62); Osprey 59 and 174 (max 150-746); Honey Buzzard 163 (max 255); Sparrowhawk 48 and 46 (max 20-224); Kestrel spring max 140-239; Peregrine 172 autumn, 198 spring; Coot 150 (max 59-263); Ringed Plover 102 (max 80-459); Lapwing 70 (max 23-173); Turnstone 103 (max 913); Wood Sandpiper 100 (max 175-625); Redshank 94; Spotted Redshank 122; Common Sandpiper 90 and 438 (max 1392); Red-necked Phalarope 201; Ruff spring 71 (max 160-757), autumn 32; Little Stint 137 (max 400); Curlew Sandpiper 209 (max 518); Dunlin 115 and 209 (max 41-1023); Knot 123 and 175; Snipe 54; Woodcock 64; Curlew 46; Garganey spring 329 (max 658); Teal 62; Arctic Tern 200 (max 330 autumn, 520 spring); Lesser Black-backed Gull max 102; Black-headed Gull max 26-277; Swift 150; Bee-eater spring max 520; Great Spotted Woodpecker 25; Barn Swallow spring max 264; Tree Pipit 57-79; Meadow Pipit 57-71; Yellow Wagtail 57-71; White Wagtail 42; Red-backed Shrike 63-96; Starling 47; Jay 61; Nutcracker 39-43; Waxwing 25-53; Wren 29; Dunnock 35-52; Reed Warbler 39-56; Sedge Warbler 50-89; Barred Warbler 92; Blackcap 46-75; Garden Warbler 58-102; Whitethroat 55-75; Lesser Whitethroat 43-75; Willow Warbler 62-85; Chiffchaff 86; Goldcrest 52-57; Pied Flycatcher 60-93 (spring 80); Spotted Flycatcher 65; Redstart 70-74; Robin 56-60 (spring 41); Bluethroat 41; Whinchat 81; Wheatear 110; Fieldfare 33; Blackbird 36-43; Song Thrush 61-69 (max 762); Redwing 47-65 (max 625); Long-tailed Tit 25-40; Willow Tit 10-23; Coal Tit 31-33; Blue Tit 12-28; Great Tit 23-35; Treecreeper 32; Chaffinch 54-63; Brambling 41-68; Greenfinch 29-45; Siskin 40-59; Twite 28; Redpoll 28-40; Bullfinch 22; Reed Bunting 24-59: [PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)
- Methodological caveat stated by the author: ring-recovery speeds include stopovers and "the estimated speed is generally lower than the actual speed"; time at stopover vs flight is about 7:1 to 9:1 in passerines (Newton 2008, Chernetsov 2012): [PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)

Flight speeds (airspeed, not daily distance)
- Alerstam et al. 2007, PLoS Biology 5(8):e197: tracking-radar airspeeds for 138 species (0.01 to 10 kg); "Mean airspeeds among the 138 species ranged between 8 and 23 m/s"; raptors, songbirds, swifts, gulls, terns and herons in the lower part; cormorants, cranes and skuas around 15 m/s; pigeons, some waders, divers, swans, geese and ducks 15-20 m/s; diving ducks above 20 m/s; per-species values in Protocol S1 (verified): [PLoS Biology](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.0050197)
- Wader flocks leaving Banc d'Arguin in spring: ground speed averaged 24 km/h against headwinds, air speed about 41 km/h (search snippet): [Wader Study Group Bulletin](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/iwsgb/n054/p00008-p00016.pdf)

Tracking-based total migration speed
- Schmaljohann 2019, Movement Ecology 7:25 (Europe PMC full text, verified): total-speed models used 22 species/populations and 180 individuals in spring, 24 species/populations and 245 individuals in autumn; per-species total speeds in Table 1 range from 24 km/day (Linnet) to 419 km/day (Scissor-tailed Flycatcher) in spring and 24 to 349 km/day (Blackpoll Warbler) in autumn; no body-mass effect; speed higher in spring; within-species log-log slope of speed on distance 0.73 (spring) and 0.96 (autumn); European species include Northern Wheatear (Swedish population), Red-backed Shrike, Pied Flycatcher, Semicollared Flycatcher, Red-spotted Bluethroat, Barn Swallow, Willow Warbler, Tawny Pipit, Linnet, Ortolan Bunting, Great Reed Warbler, Common Cuckoo, European Roller, Snow Bunting; the tracking dataset is a supplementary file on Figshare: [Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6689889/fullTextXML); [supplementary file 3](https://springernature.figshare.com/articles/Additional_file_3_of_The_start_of_migration_correlates_with_arrival_timing_and_the_total_speed_of_migration_increases_with_migration_distance_in_migratory_songbirds_a_cross-continental_analysis/9505292)
- Common Crane (Ojaste et al. 2020, Ornis Fennica 97(1)): 18 satellite-tracked juveniles; after 7-19 days of refuelling they covered 2,420 to 5,110 km in 6-15 days without settling at stopovers (verified abstract; daily km not stated): [Ornis Fennica](https://ornisfennica.journal.fi/article/view/133962)
- Common Crane autumn stopover movements (Larsson 2016, SLU thesis): mean activity area 31.5 km2 and mean flight distance from roost 4.5 km (search snippet): [SLU thesis](https://stud.epsilon.slu.se/8804/1/larsson_e_160211.pdf)
- Soaring raptors crossing the Sahara (Mellone et al. 2012, PLoS ONE 7(7):e39833, verified): 22 individuals, 73 journeys, tagged in Spain, Italy and Sweden 2006-2010; hourly ground speed while travelling: Osprey 33.1 km/h spring, 29.7 autumn; Egyptian Vulture 26.5 spring, 29.9 autumn; daily distance increased by 9.8 km/day per m/s tailwind in spring and 18.5 in autumn; in spring Egyptian Vulture and Short-toed Eagle covered shorter daily distances than Osprey and Marsh Harrier; per-species daily distances only in Figure 5: [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0039833)
- Arctic Tern (Egevang et al. 2010, geolocators): mean southbound pace about 330 km/day, northbound about 520 km/day, total 70,900 km (search snippet): [summary page](https://bioacoustics.myspecies.info/el/node/125)
- Greylag Goose (Far East population, GPS/GSM): median travel speed 62.6 km/day in spring and 47.9 km/day in autumn (search snippet; different flyway from Europe): [Ornis Fennica PDF](https://ornisfennica.journal.fi/article/download/143127/98825/370105)
- Greater White-fronted Goose (Kölzsch et al. 2016, Oikos, 65 GPS tracks): spring migration is slower than autumn because birds feed at many successive stopovers; a blog summary claims up to 2000 km in a single day with tailwind (search snippet; the Oikos page returned 404 and the Wiley abstract 403, so these numbers are unverified): [Oikos blog listing](https://www.oikosjournal.org/blog/are-birds-faster-spring-autumn)

Stopover-scale daily movement (relevant to how far a found bird moves on the next days)
- Taylor et al. 2011, PLoS ONE (Long Point, Ontario, automated radio-telemetry, verified): 322 individuals of 7 species in a 20 × 40 km landscape; 76 (24%) relocated at least 1 km from the capture site and 22 (7%) more than 10 km; relocations reached about 30 km; 52% of stopover flights occurred 24 h or more after capture; many apparent nocturnal departures were relocations within the stopover landscape: [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0027054)
- Ohio State radio-tracking: mean total distance moved per day 2.2 km for Yellow-rumped Warbler (YRWA) and 0.9 km for Red-eyed Vireo (REVI) (search snippet; abbreviations expanded from memory): [OSU page](https://senr.osu.edu/node/2893)
- Pectoral Sandpipers (Farmer and Parent): over 90% of individuals moved less than 10 km from release site (search snippet): [USDA Forest Service PDF](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/58017.pdf)
- European Robin stopover in the Baltic (radio-tagging): 1-12 days in spring (mean 2.4 ± 0.31 SE) and 1-14 days in autumn (mean 3.4 ± 0.50); radio-tagging estimates judged superior to capture-mark-recapture (search snippet): [archive.org PDF](https://archive.org/download/biostor-232009/biostor-232009.pdf)
- Blackcap stopover in northern Spain, Cormack-Jolly-Seber estimates 3.6 to 13.6 days; a Bluethroat site showed nearly 30 days (search snippet): [Arizaga et al. 2008, Bird Study](https://www.uv.es/pitroig/publicaciones/Arizaga_et_al_birdstudy08.pdf)

Eurasian African Bird Migration Atlas and Movebank
- Launched 26 May 2022 at Ventotene by CMS; ringing data for 300 species collected over more than 100 years; for over 100 species, ringing maps are overlaid with satellite, GPS-GSM and geolocator tracks held in Movebank; four modules (connectivity, long-term change, onset of return migration for huntable species, intentional killing); co-developed by EURING and the Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior; data from over 50 organisations; downloadability not stated (verified): [MPI-AB news](https://ab.mpg.de/478164/bird-migration-atlas)
- migrationatlas.org refused the connection (ECONNREFUSED) and curl returned no response on 24 October 2026; species account content (whether speed statistics are provided) could not be verified.
- Movebank Data Repository publishes datasets under CC0 with DataCite DOIs; public studies on movebank.org carry CC0, CC BY or CC BY-NC chosen by the owner, and permission is not required for those (search snippets): [Movebank data policy](https://www.movebank.org/cms/movebank-content/data-policy); [Movebank terms](https://www.movebank.org/cms/movebank-content/general-movebank-terms-of-use)
- Dutch and Belgian open tracking datasets archived on Zenodo from Movebank: Western Marsh Harrier (H_GRONINGEN, 987,493 GPS fixes of four birds since 2012; most birds stayed within 10 km of nests when breeding and wintered in West Africa), Mediterranean Gulls (MEDGULL_ANTWERPEN), Lesser Black-backed and Herring Gulls (Zeebrugge, 92 birds), Eurasian Oystercatchers (UvA-BiTS, 6.0 million GPS positions) (search snippets): [ZooKeys data paper](https://zookeys.pensoft.net/article_preview.php?id=52570); [Zenodo marsh harriers](https://zenodo.org/records/8132304); [Zenodo gulls](https://zenodo.org/records/6599273)

### Inferences
- Suggested priors for "distance a bird can be from yesterday's sighting" (daily displacement including stopover days), derived from the Payevsky group means and the stopover studies above; these are my synthesis, not published values:
  - Nocturnal long-distance passerines (warblers, flycatchers, chats, shrikes): typical 50 to 90 km per migration day, but on a stopover day 0 to 5 km, occasional relocations up to 30 km; a bird found at a stopover site has a 25% chance of moving more than 1 km and about 7% of moving more than 10 km before leaving (Taylor et al. proportions).
  - Short-distance diurnal passerines (finches, pipits, thrushes, starlings): 35 to 65 km per migration day; thrushes and larks faster than finches.
  - Tits, irruptive species (Waxwing, Nutcracker, crossbills by analogy), partial migrants: 10 to 35 km/day; slow and site-bound once in suitable habitat.
  - Waders: 70 to 200 km per migration day, with single flights of 500 to 1400 km; on stopover, >90% stay within 10 km.
  - Raptors: 100 to 200 km per travel day (soaring species need thermals, so near zero on wet or windless days); harriers and ospreys faster than eagles and vultures.
  - Cranes: order of 300 to 500 km per travel day (my arithmetic from Ojaste et al.: 2,420-5,110 km in 6-15 days), with multi-week staging periods and daily foraging radius about 4.5 km from the roost.
  - Geese and swans: tens of km per day on average in spring (Greylag median 63 km/day), with rare single-day jumps of many hundreds of km; strongly site-faithful at staging areas.
  - Gulls and terns: gulls 25 to 280 km/day maxima from ringing; Arctic Tern 330 to 520 km/day on migration; seabirds in general can be anywhere within a few hundred km the next day.
  - Ducks: 25 to 120 km/day for dabbling ducks in autumn, Garganey in spring about 330 km/day.
- Airspeed (Alerstam) converts to an upper bound on single-night displacement: 10 m/s for a warbler gives about 36 km/h, so 8 hours of flight gives roughly 290 km; waders at 18 m/s give about 65 km/h and 500 km or more per night. Use these for the tail of the kernel, not the mode.
- Because ring-recovery speeds are averaged over stopovers, they underestimate flight-day displacement; a two-state model (stay with probability p, else jump with a flight-day distance) is more realistic than one kernel per species.

### Gaps
- No open, complete per-species km/day table for all roughly 1000 waarneming.nl species exists in the sources found; Payevsky covers 115 species and Schmaljohann about 26.
- Whether the Eurasian African Bird Migration Atlas species accounts contain speed or daily-distance statistics, and whether anything is downloadable, could not be verified (site unreachable, EURING in maintenance).
- Per-species daily distances for raptors in Mellone et al. are only in a figure; White-fronted Goose daily speeds (Kölzsch et al. 2016) not verified.
- No sources found on daily movement of gannets, skuas or shearwaters in km/day; Payevsky lists only Short-tailed Shearwater (max 167 km/day) and Grey-headed Albatross (880 km/day).

## Key question 3: Habitat preferences in machine-readable form

### Takeaway

### Inferences
- For a heatmap that only needs "where does this species like to be", a workable open stack is: AVONET `Habitat` plus `Habitat.Density` (coarse class), EltonTraits foraging strata and PelagicSpecialist (separates seabirds, water-surface feeders and canopy birds), and Storchová and Hořák habitat preferences for breeding species, mapped onto a land-cover raster (for the Netherlands, for example the open Basisregistratie Topografie or CORINE land cover; this mapping is my suggestion, no source verified it).

- Sovon's free-to-use statement covers the displayed figures and maps, not an API; a request to Sovon would be needed for gridded data.
- EuroBirdPortal data (which include waarneming.nl records via Observation International, from memory) would give week-by-30 km occurrence for Europe, but access is request-based and the 30 km grid is too coarse for a local heatmap.

## Key question 5: Behaviour of rarities after discovery (stay duration, relocation, sticky versus fly-through)

### Takeaway
The only recent formal study found (British Birds, May 2025) shows that apparent stay lengths of Nearctic landbirds are strongly inflated by observer density, so published stay lengths describe detection as much as behaviour. No open quantitative distribution of stay lengths for Dutch or British rarities was found; however, Dutch Avifauna lists first and last dates per accepted record and these show the expected pattern: Nearctic waders such as Long-billed Dowitcher often stay weeks to months (and may return in later years), Siberian warblers such as Dusky Warbler show autumn windows and regular wintering, and seabirds such as Great Shearwater are nearly all single-day records.

### Cited Findings
- British Birds, May 2025, "Observer effort and its influence on apparent stay lengths of vagrant North American landbirds": five North American landbird species; comparisons Britain vs Iceland and Isles of Scilly vs Outer Hebrides; "longer apparent stay lengths in more heavily birded regions compared to more sparsely watched ones at both the national and local level", attributed mainly to observer density and reporting networks, with ecological factors possibly also playing a role (abstract via search; the article page blocks automated access and full text is behind subscription): [British Birds](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/observer-effort-and-its-influence-apparent-stay-lengths-vagrant-north-american)
- Long-billed Dowitcher in the Netherlands (verified page): most records in spring (April-June), regular July-August and September-November, relatively often in winter; most Dutch birds are adults; the page suggests some records involve individuals returning in multiple years; 62 numbered records; example stays: 4 September 2001 to 10 March 2002 (Schouwen-Duiveland, about 6 months), 30 September 2004 to 18 April 2005 (Goes, about 6.5 months), 30 December 2003 to 25 April 2004 (Goes), 16 July to 28 August 2010 (Sluis), 2 October to 8 November 2009 (Lelystad), 14 September to 14 October 2024 (Texel): [Grote Grijze Snip](https://www.dutchavifauna.nl/species/grote_grijze_snip?all=1)
- Great Shearwater in the Netherlands (verified page): 19 accepted records; of the North Sea records 12 fall in July-November, 7 in December-February (five beach finds) and 1 in April; seven of eight example rows are single-day records (e.g. 17 Nov 1915 Noordwijk, 28 Oct 1974 Schiermonnikoog, 28 Jul 1978 Texel), one spanned 6 to 11 October 1968 (Rotterdam): [Grote Pijlstormvogel](https://www.dutchavifauna.nl/species/grote_pijlstormvogel?all=1)
- Dusky Warbler in the Netherlands (verified page): 74 numbered accepted records, all between 29 September and 4 December; wintering birds recorded almost every year since 2012; example multi-day record 29 November to 4 December 1990 (Wassenaar): [Bruine Boszanger](https://www.dutchavifauna.nl/species/bruine_boszanger?all=1)
- A Spectacled Eider on Texel in 2025 stayed almost two weeks and was entered on waarneming.nl by nearly 1,800 different people (search snippet, single anecdote): [BNNVARA Vroege Vogels](https://www.bnnvara.nl/vroegevogels/artikelen/beeldschoon-en-extreem-zeldzaam-vogelaars-massaal-naar-brileider-op-texel)
- Grey Catbirds in Britain: one stayed six days (seen on three occasions) and one nine days after a day hidden in cover (search snippet, anecdotes): [BirdGuides](https://www.birdguides.com/news/surprises-in-latest-bbrc-decisions-file/)
- Slack's "Rare Birds, Where and When" (vol. 1, passerines and near-passerines) includes histograms of length of stay for most vagrants, but the reviewer notes rarity records are "too few, too biased and too patchy for real statistical rigour" (search snippet): [BirdGuides review](https://www.birdguides.com/reviews/books/rare-birds-where-and-when-vol--1/)
- Blake 1950 found that the distribution of length of stay of banded migrants approaches a geometric progression and proposed the geometric mean as summary (search snippet): [Journal of Field Ornithology PDF](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jfo/v021n04/p0151-p0152.pdf)
- A British Birds letter on 1958-65 rarities found 45% were found at weekends, 26% on Sundays (search snippet; relevant to detection bias in stay data): [British Birds letters](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/letters-165)
- Stopover relocation (Taylor et al. 2011): 24% of tracked migrants relocated at least 1 km and 7% more than 10 km within a 20 × 40 km landscape, up to about 30 km; the authors distinguish "true stopover" from "apparent stopover" (verified): [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0027054)
- Robin stopover 1-14 days (means 2.4 spring, 3.4 autumn); Blackcap 3.6-13.6 days; Bluethroat site nearly 30 days (search snippets): [archive.org PDF](https://archive.org/download/biostor-232009/biostor-232009.pdf); [Arizaga et al. 2008](https://www.uv.es/pitroig/publicaciones/Arizaga_et_al_birdstudy08.pdf)
- Irruptive and partial migrants move slowly (34 km/day vs 51 for regular diurnal migrants; Newton median 27 km/day) and "usually move for relatively short distances, in the absence of significant body energy reserves" (verified): [Payevsky 2013 PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)
- Wintering site fidelity in a long-distance migrant: 54% of Whinchats returned to a wintering study site across two years and all reoccupied previous territories; irruptive or nomadic species show little non-breeding site fidelity (search snippets; not vagrant-specific): [DOAJ, Journal of Avian Biology 2024](https://doaj.org/article/9b47850cbe214a74a686dff0e8c10141); [Journal of Field Ornithology PDF](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jfo/v067n03/p0360-p0366.pdf)
- Gilroy and Lees (2003, British Birds) conclude that "it is the pattern of observer coverage which determines the number of vagrants discovered" (search snippet): [UEA research portal](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/vagrancy-theories-are-autumn-vagrants-really-reverse-migrants/)

### Inferences
- Sticky groups (my classification from the Dutch Avifauna examples and the stopover literature): Nearctic and Siberian waders and wildfowl on suitable wetlands (weeks to months, possible return in later years), wintering Siberian warblers and pipits (weeks), herons, egrets and gulls at food sources, irruptive finches and tits, and escapes. Fly-through groups: seabirds seen from seawatch sites (single-day records dominate), raptors and storks seen overhead, swifts, migrating flocks of cranes, and nocturnal passerine migrants on good migration nights.
- A reasonable prior for a rarity's day-to-day persistence is geometric (Blake 1950 for migrants): a constant daily probability of leaving, with the probability set per group; the Robin and Blackcap stopover means (2 to 14 days) suggest daily leaving probabilities of roughly 0.1 to 0.4 for passerine migrants in autumn, lower for waders and wildfowl in winter, near 1 for seabirds and overhead raptors. These numbers are my inference.
- When a rarity stays, it usually stays within a few km (Ohio warbler and vireo daily movement 0.9 to 2.2 km; >90% of Pectoral Sandpipers within 10 km; cranes 4.5 km from roost), so a heatmap should keep most mass within about 5 km of the last sighting for a lingering bird and spread a thin tail to 30 km for relocations.
- Published stay lengths from heavily watched areas (Scilly, Texel) are upper estimates of detectability-adjusted stays; apply a correction when using them as behaviour parameters (British Birds 2025 conclusion).

### Gaps
- No open dataset or paper giving median or mean stay length per species or group for Dutch or British rarities was found; the British Birds 2025 figures are behind a paywall and Slack's histograms are in print only.
- Relocation of the same individual between nearby sites is documented only anecdotally; no study quantifying it for European vagrants was found.
- Dutch Avifauna's terms of use for programmatic access were not found.

## Key question 6: Existing vagrant-type categorisations

### Takeaway
Lees and Gilroy's "Vagrancy in Birds" (Helm 2021) organises causes as compass errors (reverse and mirror-image misorientation), wind drift, overshooting, extreme weather, irruptions and human-driven vagrancy, then treats bird families one by one; the Netherlands has an operational rarity hierarchy via waarneming.nl's rarity labels (algemeen, vrij algemeen, zeldzaam, zeer zeldzaam) and the CDNA assessment list on dutchavifauna.nl, but no published machine-readable "vagrant type" (Nearctic, Siberian, southern overshoot) field per species was found.

### Cited Findings
- "Vagrancy in Birds" by Alexander Lees and James Gilroy, Helm (London) 2021, 400 pages, ISBN 9781472964786; opening section (about 71 pages) on causes: compass errors (reverse migration, mirror-image misorientation, migration axis), wind drift and overshooting, extreme weather, irruptions, human-driven vagrancy; remainder family by family; example: eight Nearctic heron species recorded on the Azores against four in Britain (verified BTO review): [BTO book review](https://www.bto.org/learn/about-birds/book-reviews/vagrancy-birds)
- Wikipedia summarises mechanisms as spring overshoots (often young adults, citing Lees and Gilroy), reverse migration of young birds in autumn (cited to Vinicombe and Cottridge 1996), storm and wind displacement (Nearctic birds across the Atlantic), ship assistance, and Siberian passerines wintering in Southeast Asia that reach Northwest Europe; it frames causes as endogenous (navigation errors) versus exogenous (severe weather) (verified): [Wikipedia Vagrancy (biology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vagrancy_(biology))
- Gilroy and Lees 2003 (British Birds) tested the "reverse migration shadow" and found autumn vagrancy is not limited to a 180 degree route reversal; misorientation can occur in random directions (search snippets): [UEA research portal](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/vagrancy-theories-are-autumn-vagrants-really-reverse-migrants/); [Wikipedia Reverse migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_migration_(birds))
- Lees and Gilroy 2021 (Current Biology) describe vagrancy leading to establishment of a new migration route in an Asian songbird (search snippet): [UEA eprints](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/85578)
- A British Birds letter argues most North American vagrants are carried across the Atlantic by mid-latitude weather systems (search snippet): [British Birds](https://britishbirds.co.uk/journal/article/further-thoughts-transatlantic-vagrancy-landbirds-britain-ireland)
- waarneming.nl rarity labels observed via the API: algemeen, vrij algemeen, zeldzaam, zeer zeldzaam (English: common, and presumably fairly common, rare, very rare; only "common" was observed in English) and status "inheems"/"native" (verified): [waarneming.nl species API](https://waarneming.nl/api/v1/species/2/)
- CDNA (Commissie Dwaalgasten Nederlandse Avifauna) operates under the Dutch Birding Association and the Nederlandse Ornithologische Unie; species leave the assessment list once observed on average at least twice a year over the past 30 years and move to Sovon's special-species project; in 1988 the committee handled 295 cases with 82% accepted; Red-throated Pipit and Penduline Tit were dropped from assessment on 1 January 1993 (search snippets from Limosa): [Limosa 1989 PDF](https://natuurtijdschriften.nl/pub/1028674/LIM1989062004005.pdf); [Limosa 1994 PDF](https://natuurtijdschriften.nl/pub/1028876/LIM1994067004004.pdf)
- dutchavifauna.nl list: 547 numbered species since 1800; clickable species are CDNA assessment species; former assessment species show the last year assessed; filters show all species, assessment species only, or hybrids (verified): [Dutch Avifauna list](https://www.dutchavifauna.nl/list)
- EuroBirdPortal's species list distinguishes 477 regular EU species, 32 introduced species with self-supporting populations and 58 "regular vagrants" following the EU Bird List v2024-03-11 (verified): [EBP help](https://eurobirdportal.org/ebp/en/help)
- Payevsky identifies the irruptive group explicitly (Nutcracker, Coal Tit, Long-tailed Tit, Waxwing, Bullfinch) with slow movement (verified): [Payevsky 2013 PDF](https://www.zin.ru/labs/ornithology/payevsky/pdf/18-Payevsky-2013.pdf)
- A 2018 Dutch status study uses atlas-square occupancy thresholds (very rare <1% of squares, rare 1-5%, fairly rare 5-12.5%, fairly common 12.5-25%, common 25-50%, very common 50% or more); this is a general Dutch convention and not confirmed as waarneming.nl's rule (search snippet): [Soesbergen 2018 PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Martin-Soesbergen/publication/327530674_Soesbergen_2018_Status_of_the_Dutch_Branchiopoda/links/5b9370f6299bf1473925deee/Soesbergen-2018-Status-of-the-Dutch-Branchiopoda.pdf)

### Inferences
- A per-species "vagrant type" field has to be built by hand or by rule: combine AVONET centroid longitude and latitude with range (Nearctic if the breeding centroid lies in the Americas, Siberian if east of about 60 degrees E in Asia, southern overshoot if the breeding range centroid is south of the Netherlands and the record is in spring), plus a manual irruptive flag (Waxwing, crossbills, Nutcracker, Pallas's Sandgrouse by analogy) and waarneming.nl `status` for escapes. This is my proposal, not a published scheme.
- Lees and Gilroy's cause categories map onto heatmap rules: overshoots arrive in spring from the south and tend to move on north or linger at coasts; drift and reverse-migration vagrants arrive in autumn at coasts and islands and often linger in dense cover; weather-displaced Nearctic landbirds arrive after Atlantic depressions in autumn (British Birds letter) and may stay in heavily watched areas for days; irruptives arrive in autumn waves and move slowly.
- waarneming.nl's `rarity` label is usable as a coarse rarity prior; the CDNA assessment list is a stricter "national rarity" set, and the Dutch Avifauna record counts give the historical frequency per species.

### Gaps
- No source listing which species the CDNA currently assesses as a downloadable file was found; the list page is HTML only.
- The exact rule behind waarneming.nl's rarity labels is undocumented in the sources found.
- Lees and Gilroy's family-by-family assessments (which families are vagrancy-prone) are in the book only; no open summary was found.
